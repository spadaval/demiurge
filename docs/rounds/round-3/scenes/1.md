The Lists at Quarrel Field had been laid out for horses three hundred years ago, and the city had never quite agreed to stop treating them that way. The sand was raked into a long oval. The stands went up on either side in tiers of grey timber that creaked when the crowd stamped, and the crowd had been stamping for an hour. Pennants for the Thrones hung from the rail of the high box, the Hound's gold brightest. There was no pennant for her. Somebody in the cheap seats had chalked a lopsided circle on a bedsheet and was waving it.

*That's me,* said a small voice behind her eyes, astonished. *That's my mark. Somebody drew me.*

"It's a bedsheet, Quell."

*It's my mark on a bedsheet.*

Three hundred and thirty-odd others had opinions about the bedsheet, the crowd, the smell of fried dough and horse, the pennants, and her hair, which Calidarium had steamed into something she called *presentable* and Gnash called *drowned cat*. The chorus in her head was the noise of a market at noon. She'd stopped hearing it as words two weeks ago, except when a voice came up out of the murmur, the way your own name comes out of a crowd.

*Corwin,* the murmur said all at once, and went sharp.

He came out of the far gate on foot, though they'd offered him a horse, because walking looked better. White shirt, no coat, sleeves rolled. The gilt at his throat caught the sun before the rest of him did. He raised one hand and the near stand screamed.

*Oh, he's lovely,* said Calidarium. *Kill him.*

*No killing,* said Termin. *Rule four of the Lists: the first to leave the ring, yield, or lie still for a count of ten. No killing. I've read them.*

The marshal walked them to the ring, a circle of white sand a dozen strides across, raked smooth. Corwin looked at her across it and smiled the smile from the Hound's Tooth, the one that said he'd already told this story to someone and they'd laughed.

"How many are in there today?" he said. "I'd like to know who I'm beating."

"Enough to know your shirt's on inside out."

It wasn't. He looked down anyway, and the near stand laughed, and three hundred voices in her head made a sound like a cheer heard through a wall. That was the easy part. She'd always been good at the easy part.

The marshal dropped the flag.

Corwin didn't move for a breath. Then gold came off him, the way heat comes off a road in August: a shimmer, then shapes in the shimmer, then three long hounds made of light, running low across the sand at her with no sound at all.

*LEFT,* said two hundred voices. *RIGHT,* said a hundred. *Jump,* said the Drowned Choir, in harmony, which was no use to anyone.

She went left. The first hound went through the place she'd been and she felt the heat of it down her whole right side, like opening an oven. She threw out her hand and asked, and Termin gave her a line, a hard grey edge of *here and no further*, and the second hound hit it and broke apart in sparks. The stands went up. She felt the surge come into her through the soles of her feet, three hundred small powers all leaning forward at once, and for a second she was strong.

Then the third hound took her across the back of the legs and she was on the sand.

*Up up up up,* said Mislaid. *I've found your feet, they're right there.*

She got up. Corwin was walking around the edge of the ring, unhurried, letting the crowd see his face. More hounds came off him. Five. Seven. He wasn't even sweating.

She fought the way she'd fought the drain-wraith eight days ago, with everything at once and nothing in particular. Calidarium's steam to blind them, which blinded her too. A thimble's worth of lost luck from Mislaid, which made a hound stumble once. The Choir sang a note that cracked the air and made two of them fly apart, and then the Choir were spent and silent and she felt the gap where they'd been like a missing tooth. Every boon was a coin from a purse that had never been full. She could feel them giving: the hearth-warm push of Old Ember at her back, Hollin closing a burn on her forearm almost before it opened, a hundred smaller things she had no names for yet, each giving a little more than it could spare.

And all of them talking.

*Duck. Don't duck. Go for him, not the dogs. The dogs ARE him. Use the steam again. Don't use the steam. Make a joke, the crowd likes you when you make a joke. Bite him,* said Gnash. *Bite him in the leg.*

She'd been a clerk for seven years. She knew what a committee was for. It was for making sure no one person could be blamed for anything, and it was not for fighting.

A hound hit her square in the chest and the white sand of the ring went past under her and she was one stride from the edge, on her back, with sand in her mouth. The crowd made the long falling *ohhh* of people who have bet on the other side and are getting their money's worth.

She could feel the surge going out of Chat like a tide. Not boredom, not yet. Pity. Three hundred small gods watching their warlock lose in front of the city and starting, one by one, to look away so they wouldn't have to see it.

*Merrit.*

It wasn't the murmur. It was one voice, and it came up out of the crowd in her head the way Benefice's voice always did: warm, a little amused, close, like someone leaning down to speak in your ear at a party.

*You don't have to do it like this,* Benefice said. *All of them pulling in different directions. Let me hold the reins for a moment. Just this once.*

Corwin had stopped walking. He was standing over the ring's edge, and the hounds were standing with him, panting light, waiting for him to send them.

"Yield," he said, quite kindly, for him. "It's no shame. You were very funny."

*Careful,* someone said, very quietly. Nobody-in-Particular. It was the second word she had ever heard from that seat.

*Careful of what?* Benefice laughed. *Of winning? Merrit. Look at them.*

She looked at the stands. Six thousand faces. The bedsheet with Quell's mark on it had gone down. Somebody had sat on it.

*Yes,* she said. *Please.*

It came into her like the first warm minute of a bath.

Not a coin from a purse: a river. It filled her arms to the fingertips and she was on her feet without remembering standing. For a second Chat went completely silent, all three hundred, the way a room does when a stranger walks in who is much too well dressed. Then they understood what was happening to her, and the noise came back as a roar.

The hounds came. She didn't think about which way. She opened her hand and the light that came out of it wasn't gold, and wasn't any colour she had a word for, just *bright*, and the first hound simply wasn't there. Nor the second. Nor the next four. Corwin stepped back and threw everything he had, and she felt it hit her and slide off like rain off oilcloth, and she walked through it across the ring with the sand smoking under her boots.

He was good. She'd give him that afterwards, when she could think. He didn't run. He stood his ground at the far edge of the circle and put both hands up and pulled the Hound's gold around himself like a coat, and she reached into the middle of it with a hand that didn't feel like hers and *pushed*.

He went backwards out of the ring, ten feet through the air, and landed on the raked sand outside it on his back.

The Lists went mad. The stands shook. People were standing on the benches, throwing hats, screaming her name, a name half of them had learned that morning. And in her head the roar went on and on and got bigger, because it wasn't only Chat now. She felt them coming. New seats, dozens at a time, Least from every corner of the city who'd been told by someone, who'd been told by someone, and come to look. Hello. Hello. Is this her? Is this the one?

*Four hundred,* said Termin, somewhere very far off, reading it out as he always did. *Four hundred and fifty. Five hundred.*

The power was going out of her as fast as it had come, and it left her cold. Not tired: cold, as if she'd climbed out of the sea in April. Her fingers ached with it.

Corwin hadn't got up.

He was on his back on the sand with his heels drumming, and both his hands were at his throat. She saw the gilt there first, the band under the skin, and that it was brighter than it had been. Then she saw it was narrower. It was drawing in. His face was going dark above it, and his mouth was open, and no sound came out of it at all.

The crowd thought it was part of the show. They were still cheering.

"He can't breathe," Merrit said, to no one, to six hundred powers and six thousand people. "Somebody. He can't breathe."

She went to her knees beside him. Up in the high box the Hound's gold pennant hung straight down. The wind had dropped. Corwin's eyes found hers, and she saw that he wasn't angry with her. He was frightened. She hadn't known that face could do that.

*Hollin,* she said.

*I can't,* said Hollin, miserable. *Not that. It's a Throne's mark, Merrit, it's his patron, I can't get between them —*

Then the gilt loosened. All at once, the way a hand opens when it's finished with something. Corwin dragged in a breath that sounded like tearing cloth, and rolled onto his side, and coughed sand. The marshal arrived, and the marshal's men, and somebody took her elbow and lifted her up and turned her round to face the stands, and held her arm up in the air, and the noise broke over her like weather.

Corwin got to his feet on his own. He didn't look at her. He walked out the far gate the way he'd come in, on foot, with one hand at his collar.

*Six hundred and ten,* said Termin.

She stood in the middle of the Lists with her arm in the air and nearly twice as many voices as she'd had that morning, and none of them would stop telling her she was wonderful. Quell was crying with happiness. Gnash had been right, he said, he'd been right all along, he'd *said* bite him. Somewhere in there Old Ember was saying nothing at all, which was unusual enough that Merrit noticed it.

*Thank you,* she said to Benefice.

*My pleasure,* said Benefice. *Truly. There's a great deal more where that came from.*

It was nearly an hour later, in the tack room under the north stand, with Bet holding a wet cloth to the back of her neck and the noise outside still going, that Termin spoke to her alone. He managed it, with six hundred and ten listening, by being so dry that nobody else wanted to.

*A matter of record,* he said. *Two hundred and seventy-eight seats were taken today. Eleven of them are no longer held by whoever took them.*

"Then who holds them?"

*They were given up within the hour. For a consideration.* A pause, the scratch of a stone god writing in a ledger only he could see. *All eleven are now held by Benefice. It's quite allowed. The Charter says nothing about it. I only note it.*

"Eleven," said Merrit. Her fingers were still cold. Bet was looking at her, the way Bet looked at her now when she talked to nobody. "Out of six hundred. Termin, that's nothing."

*Yes,* said Termin. *I only note it.*
