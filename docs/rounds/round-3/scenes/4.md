The fire had been lit every night since the second of June. Merrit had made sure of it. She'd bought coal by the sack from the chandler downstairs, who had started calling her *the lady with the hearth* in a voice that meant he thought she was a little mad, and she'd learned to bank it before bed the way Ember told her, so there'd be red in it at morning. It was the first thing she did when she came in. Before her boots, before the kettle.

Tonight she came in at eight with the light still high and gold over the roofs, because it was the evening before Midsummer Eve and the sun wouldn't go down till nearly ten. Her feet hurt from ten days of walking the city. Her head was full. Fourteen hundred of them now, and she'd stopped being able to feel the edges of the crowd. It was like standing on the shore and trying to feel the edges of the sea.

Ilse had gone home an hour ago with a satchel of papers and a face like a closed door, saying *sleep*, which Ilse said the way other people said *goodbye*. Agathe had gone to bed early in Bet's room. The flat was quiet, except for the fourteen hundred.

She knelt and raked the grate and put coal on, and the fire came up, and she sat back on her heels and said, "Well. Tomorrow."

*Tomorrow,* Ember agreed.

Merrit had been putting off the question for a week. It had sat in her like a stone in a shoe the whole time she walked, every doorknocker and weathervane and ferry-stair, every new voice saying *hello, is this her?* She asked it now because there was no more time to put it off.

"Ilse says when a power testifies before the Court, it swears," she said. "She says there's a form of words. On its essence. What does that mean?"

The fire talked to itself for a while.

*It means what it sounds like,* said Ember. *A Throne swears on what it is, and if it lies, the oath unmakes it. That's why they never testify. Too much to lose. But the swearing itself costs, even when you tell the truth. You put your essence on the table, so to speak, in front of the Court, and the Court takes a weight of it as surety. For a Throne it's a scratch.* A pause. *For me it's the lot.*

Merrit had known. Some part of her had known since the night Ember said *I've a little to spend*, and the fire had sunk while she talked, and hadn't come back up all the way for days.

"No," she said.

*Child.*

"No. We'll find something else. Amsel's note. My mother can swear. Ilse can put the ledger in. I'm not —" Her voice went up and she brought it down, because Agathe was asleep through the wall. "I'm not lending you my mouth to kill you with."

*You don't get to decide that,* said Ember, quite kindly.

"I do, actually. It's my mouth. It's my person. A patron can't speak through a warlock who won't let her, Termin told me so. I won't let you."

*Termin,* said Ember.

*It's correct,* said Termin, and he sounded, for once, unhappy to be correct. *The warlock may refuse the use of her person to any patron. Except —*

"Except by poll," said Merrit. Her stomach dropped. "A binding poll. On my person. But that needs seven days' notice, and you'd have to post it, and you —"

She stopped.

*I posted it seven days ago,* said Ember. *On the twelfth. In the afternoon, when we were on the ferry-stairs at Gannet Reach and you were listening to that old ferry-god tell you about the bridge that put him out of work. You'd just passed a thousand. You didn't notice. Nobody notices what the stewards post. Termin kept it.*

*I kept it,* said Termin. *She asked me to. The terms allow it.* Very stiffly: *I'm sorry.*

Merrit sat on the hearthrug with her mouth open.

"You planned this," she said.

*I'm old, child, not stupid. I've known since the night I told your mother what I'd have to do. It was only a matter of getting there before you'd thought of a way to stop me.* The fire popped. *The poll reads: Merrit Foy shall lend her voice to Old Ember before the Court of Seals. Termin will take it now. It's the hour.*

"And if I refuse it after it carries? Then the pact's forfeit. Then the Undertow has me at the tide, and none of it was any use."

*Yes,* said Ember. *So you won't.*

She was right. That was the worst of it. Merrit put her face in her hands.

*The poll is open,* said Termin, in his formal voice, the one he used for the eel and the dark hours and the noon he struck Benefice from the rolls. *One thousand four hundred seats. The steward Old Ember abstains, as the matter concerns her. More than half carries.*

Merrit felt it start. She'd felt polls before, a pressure in the chorus like weather changing, voices tipping one way or another. This was different. It wasn't argument. It went through the fourteen hundred like a hush going along a church, one voice after another, and it was the same word every time.

*Aye,* said Hollin, softly.

*Aye,* said Calidarium, and her voice cracked, which Merrit had never heard it do. *Damn you, old woman. Aye.*

*Aye,* sang the Drowned Choir, in harmony, and for once it was exactly right.

*Aye,* said Mislaid. *I'll find you after. I'll find you, I always do.*

The ferry-god. The doorknocker. The weathervane, who'd only taken a seat two days ago and had barely spoken. A hundred new voices from the drowned parishes under Tallowgate, who'd never met Ember until last week and had sat up with her every night since, listening to her talk about the marsh. Aye, and aye, and aye.

*Nay,* said Quell.

The chorus went quiet around it.

*Nay,* Quell said again, very small and very fierce. *I don't care. I know it's going to carry. I'm saying nay anyway. Somebody has to.*

*Thank you, little one,* said Ember. *That's right. Somebody does.*

*Carried,* said Termin, after what felt like a long time. *One thousand three hundred and ninety-seven, aye. Two, nay. One abstaining.* He paused. *The other nay was a weathervane. It didn't understand the question. I've explained it to it since.*

Merrit laughed, which she hadn't expected, a wet ugly laugh into her own hands. It was so like Termin. Then she was crying properly, and the fourteen hundred let her, and nobody said anything for a while.

It was the first poll, she thought, when she could think, that had ever been a gift. The eel had been a joke. The dark hours had been a door shut in her face. Benefice's had been a net. This one they'd given away. Every one of them had spent their vote on something they didn't want, because Ember had asked them.

When she lifted her head the fire had burned down to a good steady red, and the window had gone the deep blue of a June night at last.

"All right," she said. "All right. Then you'll tell me something, since you've had your way."

*If I can.*

"Tell me what it was like. Sallowmarsh. Before. You've told me the worst night it ever had. Tell me an ordinary one."

The fire was quiet a moment. When Ember spoke again her voice had changed. It was softer, further back, the voice of someone talking with their eyes shut.

*Winter,* she said. *The best of it was winter. The marsh froze out to the dyke and the light came off it blue, and they'd all come in to me by four o'clock because there was nowhere else in the house that was warm. Your great-grandmother Odile, sitting nearest with her feet on my fender, mending nets, because she never stopped being a fisherman's wife whatever she'd bargained for. There was a cat. There was always a cat. A different one every ten years and all of them called Biscuit. There was an eel barrel in the corner that smelled, and nobody ever moved it.*

*Your grandmother Lisette learned her letters on my stone, with a burnt stick, because Odile wouldn't waste paper. They're still there, if you look. Under the crack. An L and an F, very crooked. And your mother.* The voice warmed. *Your mother at six, lying on her stomach on the rug with her chin in her hands, watching the coals, telling me which ones were castles. She'd tell me everything. They all did. A hearth hears everything said in a kitchen in a hundred years, you know. Every quarrel. Every proposal. Every woman who came down at two in the morning because she couldn't say it to anyone with a face.*

*I warmed four of them,* said Ember. *Four Foy women, and their men, and their cats called Biscuit, and the girl who did the scullery and cried over a boy from the drainage gang, and a tinker once who stole a spoon. I warmed them all. That's all I ever was for. It's a good thing to have been for.*

Merrit sat with her knees pulled up and her arms around them and listened, and outside the window Cantle went on with its Midsummer Eve's eve: a drunk singing on Tallowgate, a cart, somebody's door. Fourteen hundred small gods listened with her, and not one of them said a word.

*When it's done,* Ember said at last, *after, I mean, find my stone. It'll be in the drainage works at Sallowmarsh, down under the culverts somewhere. Your Mislaid can find it, I expect. Bring it here. Put it under a fire.* A pause. *Not for me. I'll not be in it. But a stone that's been a hearth that long oughtn't to be cold. Something might like it.*

"I'll find it," Merrit said. "I promise."

*Good girl.* The fire shifted and settled. *Now bank that, the way I showed you, and go to bed. You've a long day tomorrow, and you'll be doing all the talking.*

Merrit banked the fire. She did it exactly the way she'd been shown, ash drawn over the red, the little iron door at the back pushed half to. Then she sat a while longer in the dark, in front of the faint warmth of it, not wanting to go.

*Bed,* said Ember.

"Yes," said Merrit. "All right. Goodnight, Ember."

*Goodnight, child,* said the fire. *There you are.*
