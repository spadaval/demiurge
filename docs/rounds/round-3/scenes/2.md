By nine in the morning Merrit had read the physician's note so many times that she could see it with her eyes shut, which was a mercy, because her eyes kept shutting.

*Frost on the cradle-rail, in April. Smell of brine. Fever without cause.*

It lay on the kitchen table between a cold cup of tea and the heel of a loaf, a single leaf cut out of a casebook with Ilse's penknife, in a dead man's crabbed brown hand. Twenty-five years in a drawer in Seal Street. She'd brought it home under her shirt, against her skin, as if someone might try to take it back.

Tomorrow at noon the chorus would vote on whether she belonged to the Undertow. She ought to be sleeping. She ought to be planning. Instead she sat in the kitchen with four hundred small gods behind her eyes, every one of them looking at the same eleven words.

*Brine,* said Calidarium, for perhaps the twentieth time. *In a nursery. In April.*

*We've read it,* said Termin.

*I'm reading it again.*

Quell had stopped asking what it meant around midnight and had gone quiet in the way Quell went quiet, like a chalk mark in a light rain. Old Ember hadn't said a word since the Grey Room. Merrit had stopped asking her to.

*There's a thing I should note,* said Termin.

"You always should."

*Nobody-in-Particular's seat went dark at four this morning.* A pause. *Not left. Dark. As if it had been cut. Under the terms an unheld seat is struck at the next audit, which is at sunrise tomorrow.* Another pause, drier. *We are three hundred and ninety-nine.*

She thought about that small, steady seat. The two words a week apart, both of them *careful*. The boons that came when she was too tired to ask for anything: never large, never showy, a little coolness on a burn, a little steadiness in her knees. She'd thought, if she thought about it at all, that it was some shy old thing that liked her.

Something was coming up the stairs.

It wasn't a knock. It was the sound of something wet being dragged one step at a time, and a breath between each step that sounded like it was being pulled through cloth. The four hundred went silent in her head at once. Merrit was on her feet with a kitchen knife in her hand before she'd decided to stand up. Seven weeks ago that sound had come up from the cellar drain and Saltlick had died in front of it.

She opened the door.

Her mother was sitting on the third step from the top, holding the banister with both hands.

Agathe Foy was soaked to the skin. Her good grey wool coat hung off her like weed off a piling, and her salt-white hair, which Merrit had never once seen out of its pins, lay down her back in a wet rope. There was a smear of green river-mud along one cheekbone. Her lips were blue. As Merrit stared she bent forward and coughed, and water came out of her: not spit, water, a cupful of it, brown and smelling of the Brack, splashing on the step between her shoes.

"Don't," said Agathe, when Merrit came down to her. "Don't fuss. Help me up."

Merrit helped her up. Her mother weighed nothing. She'd always seemed so tall.

She got her to the chair by the grate, the cold grate nobody used, because the chimney smoked and Bet had always said it wasn't worth the coal. She pulled the wet coat off her mother's shoulders and put Bet's old blanket round them instead. Agathe let her. That frightened Merrit more than the water.

*Hollin,* she said.

*Yes,* said Hollin, already there. Merrit felt the small warmth go out through her own hands, into her mother's back, down into the lungs. Agathe stiffened at the touch.

"I can feel that," she said. "Which one is it?"

"Saint Hollin. Of Small Mercies. He's helping."

"I know who Hollin is." Agathe shut her eyes. "Thank him for me."

*She can't hear us,* said Calidarium, wondering. *Can she? She's a Throne's warlock, she shouldn't be able to feel any of us at all.*

Merrit put the kettle on, because her hands needed something to do. When she turned round her mother was looking at the table. At the leaf of paper on it.

"What happened to you?" Merrit said.

"I was in the bath." Agathe's voice was very hoarse. "At four this morning. I couldn't sleep. The water in the tub came up over the edge, and then it kept coming. It came up the drain and out of the taps and through the floorboards, and it was the river, and it was cold." She coughed again, more gently. "It held me under. Not long enough to drown me. It knows exactly how long that is. Then it let me go, and it told me why."

"Why?"

Agathe looked at her. In all her life Merrit had never seen her mother look uncertain about anything. She did now.

"Because I've been stealing from it," Agathe said. "Since your birthday. A thread. A very little of what it gives me, every day, enough to sit somewhere it hadn't told me to sit." She drew the blanket closer. "Careful, Merrit."

She said it softly, and drily, exactly as she'd said it at Saltlick's wake, and again at the Lists, with Merrit flat on the sand and six thousand people screaming.

The kettle began to shriek. Neither of them moved.

In Merrit's head the four hundred came apart like a dropped tray. *Her? HER? She was in here. She was in here the whole time. She heard everything. She saw the bath.* Gnash's old seat had been sold weeks ago, but someone with his manners said *spy*, and someone else said *mother*, and Quell said, delighted and appalled, *you have a MOTHER,* as if it were a thing Merrit had been keeping in a drawer.

Merrit took the kettle off the hob. Her hand was not quite steady.

"The bath," she said.

"I looked away," said Agathe. "When the saint asked. I wasn't the only one."

"You called them a mob. You sat in that chair three weeks ago and called them a mob of starving things."

"Yes."

"You were one of them."

"Yes," said Agathe. "I was starving." She said it as plainly as she said everything, as if it were a line on a form. "Twenty-five years, Merrit, and I had never once seen what you did with it. With your life. I paid for it and then I wasn't let near it. You went to that clerk's desk and you hated me and you were right to. And then you made this —" one hand moved, a small gesture at the air above Merrit's head, at all of them "— and anyone could watch you. Any old hearth, any milestone. So I took a seat." Her mouth twisted. "I gave it the stupidest name I could think of. I didn't think anyone would ever ask."

*Nobody ever did,* said Termin, very quietly. *I should have.*

"You weren't there," Merrit said slowly. "The night you came here. Termin noticed. Your seat was empty the whole hour."

"I couldn't be in two places. I'm not a god." Agathe's laugh turned into a cough. "And I couldn't sit in your head and watch my own face beg you. I'm not brave enough for that."

"Why careful?" said Merrit. "Benefice. Why did you say careful, the first day? Nobody else suspected anything."

Her mother was quiet for a moment.

"Because it tasted like me," she said. "What it gave you. The others, your small things, they're all different, they taste of chalk or soap or old stone. Benefice tasted of salt. Warm going in, and then cold after, like getting out of the sea." She looked at her own hands, the blue going out of the nails. "That's what it's been like to be me, every day, since I was twenty-five. I knew it the moment it came in. I'd have known it anywhere."

"And you didn't tell me."

"What would I have said? That my patron had taken a seat in my daughter's chorus? I wasn't sure. And I couldn't speak against it without it knowing I was there." Her voice dropped. "And I didn't know what it wanted. I thought it wanted you back. I thought, if it has you, at least you'll live. I didn't understand until it posted that poll that it wants all of them. All of your small things, swallowed through you, like a net drawn up." She looked up. "I'd have voted for it, Merrit. Tomorrow. That's the truth. I'd have voted you into its hands to keep you breathing. I'm glad it took the seat first."

Merrit stood in her own kitchen with the steam from the kettle going up past her face, and found that she couldn't hate her for it. She had tried for years. She tried now. It wouldn't come.

She picked up the leaf of paper from the table.

"I need you to read something," she said. "We took it out of the Exchange the night before last. It was in our family file. Dr Amsel's casebook."

Agathe's face went still at the name.

Merrit put the note into her mother's hands. Agathe held it a long way from her face, the way she held everything now, and read it. Merrit watched her mouth move on the words. *Frost on the cradle-rail, in April.* She watched her read it again.

"Amsel drank," said Agathe. "By the end of that year he drank all day."

"Did he?"

Agathe didn't answer. Her hands had started to shake, a fine tremor that made the paper whisper.

"There was frost," she said at last, so low that Merrit had to lean in to hear it. "I remember. I thought it was my own fear. I thought I was going mad from not sleeping. I put my hand on the rail and it was cold enough to burn, and the window was open on an April night and there were primroses outside it." She stopped. "The whole house smelled of the sea, and the sea was nine miles off."

"Mum," said Merrit. She hadn't called her that since she was sixteen.

"I didn't let myself think about it." Agathe's voice cracked. "It saved you. That was the only thing I let myself know. It saved you, and so it was owed, and so I paid. If it wasn't —" She couldn't finish.

"Who else was in the room?" Merrit said. "That night. Who else would have seen?"

"No one. The nurse had gone to bed. Your grandmother was in Cantle. It was just me." Agathe looked down at the paper, at the cold grate in front of her chair, at nothing. "Just me and the fire."

In Merrit's head, out of four hundred voices gone quiet, one spoke. It was dry and warm and very tired, and Merrit hadn't heard it in ten days.

*I was the fire,* said Old Ember.

Merrit sat down hard on the edge of the table.

All this time she'd never once asked Ember what house she'd been the hearth of. Two months of that voice at her back, *well, there you are*, keeping her warm, and she'd never asked where Ember came from.

"Ember," she said aloud. Her mother looked up sharply. "Tell her. Tell us."

*Tonight,* said Ember. *Let me find it first. It's a long way down, and I've been keeping it under so long I'd forgotten I was. Light the grate tonight, child. I'll tell you both.*

Agathe was watching her face. "What did it say?"

Merrit looked at her mother, wrapped in Bet's blanket in the chair by a fireplace no one used, with the river still drying in her hair.

"It said she was there," Merrit said. "And it said to light the fire."
