The grate smoked, as it always had. Bet used to say it wasn't worth the coal, and for two years nobody had argued. Merrit knelt in front of it at eleven at night with a scuttle of coal she'd bought at the chandler's downstairs and a twist of newspaper, and watched the smoke come back out into the room instead of going up.

"It's always done that," she said. "The chimney's wrong."

*The chimney is perfectly good,* said Old Ember. *There's a little iron door at the back, above the fire, on the left. Somebody's shut it. Reach up.*

Merrit reached up into the soot and found it: a little iron flap, stiff with rust. She forced it. The smoke turned round in the grate as if it had remembered something and went up the flue, and the newspaper caught, and then the coal.

"How did you know that?"

*I'm a hearth,* said Ember. *Or I was.*

Agathe sat in the chair she'd sat in all day, in a dress of Bet's that was too short for her and Merrit's own cardigan over it. She had slept for most of the afternoon in Bet's empty room and woken saying nothing. Now she watched the fire come up with her hands in her lap and her face very still. She couldn't hear Chat any more. The thread the Undertow had cut that morning had been her only way in.

All three hundred and ninety-nine were there. Merrit could feel them crowded behind her eyes, the way you can feel a room full of people in the dark. For once they weren't talking. Termin had asked them not to, in his driest voice, *the stewards request silence*, and for once they'd listened.

*I can't tell it through you,* Ember said. *Not passed along. Not your words for mine. She has to hear it from me.*

"She can't hear you."

*She could if you lent me your mouth.*

Merrit sat back on her heels. She'd felt Chat do it before, a little. At the Hound's Tooth they'd fed her lines and her tongue had run ahead of her. That had been a game. This was different.

*It costs,* Ember said, before she could ask. *Me, not you. I'll spend a little. I've a little to spend.* A pause. *Please, child.*

"All right," Merrit said. "Yes."

It felt like stepping back from a window to let someone else look out. Her jaw moved without her. Her breath went in deep, deeper than she'd have taken it, the breath of someone settling into a chair they knew. And when her mouth opened, the voice that came out wasn't hers. It was low and dry and old, with the marsh in its vowels, like someone who'd been born before the drainage and never forgiven it.

"Agathe," said Old Ember. "Lisette's girl. Look at you, in a borrowed frock."

Agathe's head came up as if she'd been struck.

"I know," said Ember, gently, out of Merrit's mouth. "It's a shock. You'll want proof. Well. The night before your mother took you to the river stairs, you came down to the kitchen at two in the morning and knelt on my stone in your nightgown and said to me, out loud, because there was nobody else to say it to: I won't do this to a daughter of mine. I'll die first. Then you went up to bed. I don't suppose you remember."

Agathe had gone white to the lips.

"Nobody heard that," she said. "Nobody was there."

"I was there," said Ember. "I was always there. That's what a hearth is."

Merrit, inside her own head, stood very still behind her eyes and listened.

"You were twenty-five," Ember went on. "And three years later you came home to Sallowmarsh to have the baby, because Foy women always had, in the big front bedroom over my kitchen. Seven days old she was on the night I'm telling. April. You'd opened the nursery window because it was warm, and the primroses were out along the drain. And the house had a well, Agathe. You knew that. Under the scullery floor. Your grandmother had it boarded over when it went brackish. The Brack's under all that marsh, child, and the Brack goes down a long way, and the old well went down to it."

The fire popped. Agathe didn't move.

"I felt it before you did. About midnight. I felt cold coming up under the house the way you'd feel a draught under a door. Not night-cold. Deep cold, sea-cold. It came up the old well and in under the scullery and along under the boards to the nursery, the way water finds the low places, and it was looking for something. And it found her."

Merrit felt her own hands, the ones Ember was borrowing, close on her knees.

"The frost came up the legs of the cradle. On the rails. In April. And she burned. You know that part, you held her. Hot as a coal and then not hot, going cold from the feet up, and you sat with her and you thought you'd gone mad. I couldn't tell you. Who hears a hearth? So I did the only thing I could do. I put my heat into that room. All of it. I drew so hard the coal went in an hour, and then I burned what I was. I cracked my own stone doing it. You'll find it there still, if you ever look, a crack from the front edge to the back. I kept her warm from midnight to dawn with nothing in me but what I was made of, and it wasn't enough. I could feel it not being enough."

Her voice, Merrit's voice, had begun to thin. It came up out of her chest with an effort now.

"And at dawn you called on your patron," Ember said. "You did. I heard you. You got down on your knees by the cradle and you called on the Undertow to save her, because it was the only power you had. And it answered. It was already there, Agathe. It had been there all night. It spoke up out of the cold in the floor, and it named its price, and you said yes before it had finished. And the cold went back down the well. All at once, like a hand taken off a throat. And she was warm. Not warmer. Warm, that minute, pink, crying for the breast."

There was a long silence. The fire talked to itself in the grate.

"It didn't save her," said Ember. "It let go of her. That's all. It let go of her, and you paid it for letting go."

Agathe made a sound Merrit had never heard a person make. She bent forward in the chair over her own lap, as if something had hit her low in the stomach, and her hands went over her face, and her salt-white hair came down out of the pins Merrit had lent her.

"I sold you," she said through her hands. "Merrit. I sold you to the thing that was killing you. I thanked it. Every year on your birthday I thanked it."

Merrit wanted her mouth back. She wanted to say something. Ember, very gently, didn't give it to her yet.

"You were tired and frightened and twenty-eight and alone in a cold house," Ember said. "It chose that night for a reason. It had chosen your mother's night, too, I'd wager, and her mother's. That's how it's always done it. It makes the hurt and then it stands in the doorway selling the cure."

*The wraith,* Merrit thought, and the thought went through the whole chorus at once like a shiver, three hundred and ninety-nine small gods understanding the same thing. The drain-water coming up the cellar stairs in the middle of the night, and Saltlick going quiet. And the very next day, at the wake, warm and witty and generous, Benefice. *Let me hold the reins. Just this once.* She'd thanked it, too.

*Yes,* said Ember, inside, to her alone. *Like that.*

Then Ember stepped back from the window, and Merrit had her own mouth again. It felt raw, as if she'd been shouting. Her jaw ached.

"Mum," she said.

Agathe didn't take her hands from her face. Merrit got up off the hearthrug on legs that had gone to sleep and went to her, and knelt by the chair, and after a while put her arms round her mother's shoulders. Agathe was shaking. She smelled of the river still, under Bet's borrowed soap. Merrit couldn't remember the last time she'd held her. She wasn't sure she ever had.

*Ember,* she said, inside. *Why didn't you tell me? Two months. You were the first voice I ever heard. You could have said.*

It was a long time before Ember answered, and when she did she sounded very tired, and further off than before.

*They pulled my house down when you were twelve,* she said. *You'll not remember. A drainage works. I had no stone any more, child, nothing to burn in, and a hearth with no house goes to pieces quicker than you'd think. I forgot things. I forgot most things. I kept one. I kept you. I followed you into the city, and every cold room you lived in, I was in the chimney, doing what I could. It wasn't much. I was there in the walls of every place you slept, and I'd forgotten why. Only that I had to be.*

*So at the crossroads —*

*I was already there. I'm always nearest. That's all it was.* A dry little sound, almost a laugh. *And then in that dark place you said frost, and it all came up at once, the whole night of it, and I couldn't hold it and talk too. I've been holding it since. It's a long way down. It took me till this morning to reach the bottom.*

Merrit put her face against her mother's shoulder.

"It's fraud," she said aloud, into the wool. Her voice came out cracked and ordinary and her own. "Mum. Do you understand? That's fraud. Under the Charter. Wyck wrote it. A pact got by fraud is void, and everything made under it, all the way back. Yours. Grandmother's. All of it. Not just mine."

Agathe lifted her head. Her face was wet and blotched and older than Merrit had ever seen it, and there was something in it that hadn't been there all day, or for twenty-five years.

"Who would they believe?" she said. "Me? I'm its warlock. Amsel's dead and drank. A note about frost."

"Her," said Merrit. "A party to a registered pact has standing in the Court of Seals. She's a party. She's in my pact. She's a steward of it." She turned toward the grate. "Ember. Would you say it again? To them?"

The fire had sunk while they talked. It was a heap of red now, not much more, breathing in the draught from the little iron door.

*Yes,* said Ember. Just that.

Agathe slid out of the chair onto her knees on the hearthrug, the way, Merrit understood, she'd knelt once on a kitchen stone at two in the morning in her nightgown. She put her hand out toward the fire, not quite close enough to burn.

"Thank you," she said. "Thank you. I didn't know you were there."

The coals flared up once, bright, as if someone had breathed on them. Then they settled.

Behind Merrit's eyes the silence held, three hundred and ninety-nine of them still keeping it. And then, very small, because Quell had never been able to keep a silence in its life:

*Is she ours now too?*

Merrit looked at her mother, kneeling at the hearth in a borrowed dress with her hand held out to the fire.

"I think she might be," she said.
