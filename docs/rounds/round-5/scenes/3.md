They made camp that night under the wall of a ruined lock-house above Fennick, where the towpath bent away from the river and there was a little dry ground and a hawthorn to break the wind. Twelve days out of Saltcombe. The barge they'd hitched with had gone on to unload at Fennick wharf, and the bargemaster had said he'd take them on in the morning if they were on the bank by first light and didn't bring the weather with them.

Kit built the fire. She was good at fires now. Brannoc had taught her to lay them on the walk upriver, in the long grey evenings when there was nothing else to teach, and she had taught him to tell the one about the three bailiffs and the goose so that a room would laugh in the right places. He still told it like a man reading out a will. She hadn't had the heart to say.

When the fire had caught he went and knelt on the far side of it, facing the dark the way he always did, and began the Walker's Hour.

She had watched him do it every night since the barge left Lanthorn, thirty-one of them. She knew it the way she knew any part she'd heard more than once, which was perfectly, without wanting to: the long first prayer for those still on the road, and the shorter one for those who'd got home, and the last, which was only a line. *Saint Iver, keep the light for those still walking.* He said it into the dark at the end of every hour and the dark never said anything back.

And every line of it, every single night, Lampwick tithed.

A mote. Never more. A warmth so small she'd stopped noticing it years ago, the way you stop noticing a clock. She had only begun to notice it again on the barge going down, and then she couldn't stop: Brannoc kneels, and somewhere at the back of the gallery a small gold light leans forward and gives what it has. Every hour. Every line. Like a nod across a room.

And then Saltcombe. The clerks coming up out of the flooded scriptorium with their pens still in their hands, and the grey names withholding all at once so that her spell went out in her fingers like a match in rain, and then a tithe coming up out of the gallery that was not a mote. That was not a hundred motes. One patron, one name in the roll, and enough power in it to stagger her, and Brannoc's shield blazing white in the abbey gate so that the clerks could not come through. He'd stood there with the light on his face looking like a man who has heard his name called across a crowded square.

He thought his god had come back, for that one minute. He'd said so on the salt flats. *He answered. At Saltcombe. He'll answer again.*

Kit sat with her knees up and her coat round them and watched the fire, and listened to him pray, and felt Lampwick tithe, one mote, one line, and thought: I know what you are.

She couldn't ask.

That was the whole trouble, and she'd been turning it over for twelve days like a coin across her knuckles. Everything she said, they heard. Everything she saw, they saw. Four hundred and two of them, and two hundred of those were grey, and whatever the grey heard went down in a book in Lanthorn in a clerk's tidy hand. If she said a name out loud by this fire, Corvin Thale would have it by breakfast.

But there was one door in the pact that only opened one way. She'd found it at fifteen, when the requests started coming: a patron could pin words to a tithe, and the words came only to her. The gallery saw a mote go. They never saw what it said. *Kiss the fiddler. Do the fall again. I liked that.* A note pushed under a door. It was the only private thing she owned, and it wasn't even hers; it belonged to whoever was pushing.

So she couldn't ask. But she could let something be asked of her.

"Lampwick," she said, low, sleepy, the voice you use to a cat. "You're keen tonight."

*Gristle: he's always keen when the big lad does his little prayers*

*Gristle: it's disgusting*

*Silt: Leave them be.*

Kit put her chin on her knees and looked at the fire, and let her face go slack and soft, the way it went when she was nearly asleep and not thinking about anything, which was a face Maud had made her practise in a mirror at nine years old because a sleeping child is the best prop in a drawing-room swindle. "Go on, then," she murmured. "Tell me something I don't know."

*Gristle: oh she's talking to her favourites now. lampwick's never said a word in its life love*

On the far side of the fire Brannoc said, *For those still on the road, and those who have lost it.* Lampwick tithed.

And there were words in it.

Not a voice. A voice would have been in the scroll for everyone. This came in the warmth itself, the way a request did, the way *do the fall again* did, only slower, and stranger, and with a weight to it she had never felt from any of them, as if each word had been carried a long way.

*You know.*

She did not move. She kept her eyes on the fire and her mouth soft and let the heat be on her face, and inside she held absolutely still, the way you hold still when a deer comes out of the trees.

Another line. Another mote.

*Say nothing aloud. They are listening.*

*I know,* she thought, uselessly; there was no door going the other way.

The prayer went on. And with each line of it, Lampwick told her.

It came in pieces, a few words a mote, so slowly that she could feel how much each one cost it. A Salt War, and burned chapter houses, and an Order that borrowed to build them again and pledged everything it had, which was a god's houses, which was the god. A morning three years ago when the loan was called. *A god whose house is pledged is a thing that is owed. Any prayer to me by my name is a prayer to my creditor.* A night in a chapter at Wendmoor, the last night it had a house, and one young knight on vigil who would not go to bed though the bailiffs' carts were in the yard.

*I gave him the last thing I had. I told him where to go.*

*Find the lantern that many hands hold.*

Kit's eyes stung. She let them; a sleepy girl's eyes water at a fire.

*Then I put down my name, and came into the dark, and you called me Lampwick, and I have answered to nothing else.*

On the far side of the fire Brannoc's voice dropped for the second prayer, the short one, for those who'd got home.

Kit yawned, a real one, half-made and half-meant, and said into her knees, slurring it: "Why him, though."

*Gristle: because he's the size of a barn door*

*Gristle: and about as bright*

*Silt: Gristle.*

Lampwick tithed.

*Fifteen and lost on the Wendmoor fen with the causeway gone under. He walked toward a light because it was the only one. It was Osric's shield. Mine.*

*He has kept every hour since. Every one. For three years he has said my name into the dark and it has gone into a ledger in the Counting House, and they have heard every word, and I have not been able to answer him once.*

She thought of the white light in the abbey gate. *But you did,* she thought. *At Saltcombe.*

As if it heard her face, it said: *I did not answer him. I tithed to you. You lit him. That is not the same. A god may give to a warlock; a creditor cannot trace a gift. But if he calls me by my name to my face, I will answer. I would not be able not to. And a god who answers is a god who is found.*

The last line of the hour was coming. She could hear it in his breathing.

*Do not tell him. He would say my name to me before he could stop himself. Here, beside you. With two hundred of them listening through your ears.*

*Will you keep it?*

She couldn't say yes. She couldn't nod; they'd see the nod and ask what it was for. She sat for a moment with the question in her, warm, a note under a door, and then she reached out and took a stick and pushed the burning wood together and heaped the ash up over the brightest of it, banking the fire for the night, the way Brannoc had taught her: so it would still be alive in the morning, and nobody passing on the road would see it.

*Saint Iver,* said Brannoc, into the dark, *keep the light for those still walking.*

Lampwick tithed one more mote.

*Thank you.*

Then it was quiet, and when she let her eyes go to the roll at the edge of her sight, its name was there in its place, among the white, and its light was lower than she had ever seen it. As if it had spent, in one hour, everything it had been saving since Saltcombe.

---

Brannoc came round the fire and lay down on the near side of it with his cloak under him and the shield beside him, close enough that she could feel the warmth of him through her coat. Since the salt flats they had slept that way. Chat had a great deal to say about it every night. She had stopped answering.

"You were talking," he said.

"To Chat."

"What did they say?"

"Nothing worth repeating." She lay down too, with her back to his chest, facing the banked fire. "Gristle thinks you're the size of a barn door."

"Gristle is not wrong." He was quiet a while. She could feel him not sleeping. Then, very low, the way a man says something he has said so often it's worn smooth: "He answered, at Saltcombe. I keep thinking: if I keep the hours, he'll answer again."

She could have said it. It was one word. She could have turned over and put her mouth to his ear so the fire couldn't hear it and given him back the thing he had walked three years for, and two hundred grey names would have heard it through her own ears, and the god at the back of the gallery would have heard its name said to its face by the one man it could not refuse, and answered, and been found.

"Maybe," she said. "Go to sleep."

He did, eventually. She didn't. She lay with her eyes open and watched the banked fire breathe under its ash, and four hundred and two of them watched it with her, and only one of them knew what she was keeping, and it was too tired now to say.

*Night, Chat,* she said at last, the way she always did.

They answered, the way they always did. All of them but one.
