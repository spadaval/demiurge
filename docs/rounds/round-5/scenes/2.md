The last thing Silt saw was the fire.

Driftwood, salt-white, burning blue at the edges where the brine was in it. The flats beyond going out grey to the line of the tide. Brannoc's face across the flames, still streaked with the clerks' ink where one had caught him. And the child's hands, Kit's hands, held out to the heat, not steady.

*I'm sorry,* Kit said, aloud, to all of them. *One night. Just one. Hold on.*

Then the Window shut, and there was nothing to see at all.

---

Silt had been dark before. She had been a ford, once, before the Marle was walled. Women had beaten their washing on her stones and drovers had walked their cattle through her at the ebb, and every night a lamp had hung on a post at either bank so that nobody crossing late would miss the stones. Then the city came and put the river in a stone coat, and nobody needed a ford, and she had lain under the Sinks for a hundred years in water that didn't move, getting smaller. She knew the dark. She had been sure she would end in it.

This dark was different. This one had four hundred and eighty-nine others in it, and all of them were screaming.

Not in voices. They had no voices without the scroll, and the scroll was still there between them, the whisper-dark where they spoke to one another, but Kit was not reading it now and so it ran on with no one at the end of it, a river with no sea. Everything at once. *Where is she. What did we do. Is she dead. Is she dead. She's dead.* Names Silt had not heard speak in seven years shrieking the same four words. Something small she didn't know crying for a bell. And under all of it, so quiet you could take it for the hum of the dark itself, the other thing: the hunger.

They had none of them understood how much they were living on her. The taste of bread. Cold on skin. The weight of a coat. Things small enough that you never noticed them coming in, and now they had stopped, and it was like the tide going out of a pool and leaving the little creatures lying in the sun.

*She's not dead,* Silt said. She said it slowly and very large, the way a river says things, and some of the noise near her stopped to listen. *She's sitting by a fire with the paladin. She shut the curtain. She told us she would. She'll open it in the morning.*

*Why,* said a voice. Thimble, who never spoke.

Silt thought of the scriptorium the day before. Kit standing in it reading aloud, a performer reading another performer's last lines, her voice going thin on the end of it: *Do not shut your eyes.*

*Because she's fourteen,* Silt said, *and she's been fourteen in front of us for eight years, and she wanted a room with a door on it. Haven't you ever wanted a door?*

That was not a good enough answer, and she knew it. It was only the true one.

---

The first ones went within the hour.

Silt felt them go the way you feel a stone shift underfoot. They were the faintest, the ones at the back of the dark who had come into Kit's gallery already half gone and lived on her the way you live on a crust, and without the Window there was nothing between them and the end of it. They did not cry out. They thinned, and then they were not there, and the place where they had been was a little colder.

Some were grey.

She had not thought of that. None of them had. But of course: the ones the buyer bought first were the hungriest, the most faded, the ones who would sign anything for a promise of more. The promise of more did not reach into the dark. Silt felt a grey name go out like a pinched wick, and then another, and then she stopped counting them and only felt them, a cold here and a cold there, and could not tell whether she was glad.

Then the offers began.

They came through the grey names, which had never spoken, and spoke now. Not in the voices the names had once had. In one voice, dry and patient, the voice of a clerk reading out a figure.

*It is noted that you are hungry.*

*It is noted that you have been left.*

*Sustenance is available. No fading. Terms on request.*

It went through the dark like the smell of bread through a poorhouse. Silt felt the gallery turn toward it. She felt one patron, somewhere off to her left, a small god of a milestone on the old Harrowgate road, lean and lean and then give, and go grey. Then another.

*Anyone who signs,* said Gristle, very loud, *I'll find.*

It had been silent since the fire. Now it came up out of the dark like a root through a floor.

*I'm a tree. You know what trees have? Time. I'll find you in a hundred years at the bottom of some Throne's ledger with your name scraped off, and I will remember you did it. I'll say your name to every crow I see.* A pause. *That's a threat. In case any of you lot are slow.*

The leaning stopped. Not all of it. But some.

---

It was not enough, Silt knew. Gristle could frighten them for an hour. It could not feed them. The hunger would win in the end, as it had won with Hollin's three hundred, who had probably had a Gristle too, and a Silt, and lost them both.

So she did the only thing she knew how to do. She did what a child had done in a vault.

*Once upon a time,* Silt said, into the screaming dark, *there was a miller's daughter who dropped her mother's ring in the race.*

Nobody answered. She went on anyway.

She told them *The Miller's Drowned Ring*, every verse, though she had to stop twice and go back for a line. She told them *The Merchant's Wager* and *The Queen of Harrowgate* and the one about the three bailiffs and the goose. They were the stories Kit had told in the Sump, the plays Maud Tallow had drilled into her, and Silt had heard every one of them that first time, lying in the black water under the Sinks with her ear to the sound of a girl's voice because it was the only living thing that had spoken near her in a hundred years. She did not have Kit's memory. She had a river's: slow, and full of mud, and she got the order wrong. It did not matter. Somewhere in the second story the screaming near her went down to talking, and in the third the talking went down to listening, and in the fourth somebody laughed at the goose.

It was not food. She had nothing to give them. But it was a voice that went on in the dark and knew what came next, and she had learned eight years ago that that was nearly as good.

Somewhere around the middle of the night she felt a light.

It was very small. The size of a candle-flame, of the wick of one, burning low down among the faintest of them at the back, where the cold was worst. It gave no heat that she could feel. But the ones nearest it had stopped thinning. She could feel them there, a dozen, twenty, gathered round it the way people gather round a lamp on a dark road and wait for whoever is coming.

*Lampwick,* she said.

It did not answer. It almost never did.

But Silt was old, and she had been a ford, and every night for six hundred years a lamp had hung on a post at either bank of her so that nobody crossing late would miss the stones. She knew that light. She knew the particular colour of it, a little more gold than a candle, and the way it seemed to wait. It was the light the lamplighters used to say a prayer over when they climbed their ladders. She had not seen it since the posts came down.

She thought she knew, then, what was sitting at the back of Kit's gallery calling itself by a name Kit had given it as a joke. And she thought she knew, too, why a thing like that would want to be called nothing else.

She said nothing. A name was a door. She was not going to be the one to open it.

*Go on,* said Gristle, out of the dark. *The goose. What happened to the goose.*

---

Toward morning, when the cold was deepest, she told them the last one.

*Once upon a time,* she said, *there was a girl in a vault.*

She told it the way Kit never did, from the outside. From the water. The bailiffs' boots on the stair, and the door going shut, and the dark. The girl not crying, the first night, because she was saving it. The girl crying the second. And on the third, beginning to talk: a play, then another, then all of them, to nobody, to the walls, to keep herself from going mad. And the small hungry things in the black water under the Sinks drifting up, one and then another, to listen.

*On the fourth night,* Silt said, *she laughed. Somebody made her. It was a rhyme. A skipping rhyme, out of the dark, the stupidest thing. Penny on the water, penny in the well—*

She stopped. She had lost the next line. It had been eight years and she had never been good with rhymes.

*One for the boatman,* said a voice. *One for the bell.*

It was very quiet. It came from among the grey.

Silt turned toward it with everything she had, and the dark was full of grey names, silent, every one of them exactly as still as the last, and she could not tell which had spoken. She waited. Nothing more came.

*One for the boatman,* Silt said, after a while. *One for the bell. And she laughed, and after that she wasn't alone in there, and she never has been since. Not one night. Not until tonight.*

She felt the gallery holding still around her, all of them, the white names and the grey.

*She'll open it,* Silt said. *And we'll be here.*

Then the Window opened, all at once, like a door into a lit room, and there was grey light on salt flats, and the ash of a fire, and the smell of the sea, and the child's face, which none of them could see because they were looking out of it, wet.
