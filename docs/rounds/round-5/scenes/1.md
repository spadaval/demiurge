Wending held its fair on the water meadow below the lock, and by the time the *Patience* had tied up behind a line of eel-boats the whole town seemed to have walked down the hill to it. There were pens of geese and a man selling knives off a blanket. There was a puppet booth with a hole in its curtain and a girl selling hot chestnuts out of a drum. Bunting had been strung between the willows and had rained wet overnight, and it hung in the cold sun like washing.

Ysolde stayed aboard with the Saltcombe charts and her cough. She had spoken to Brannoc perhaps a dozen words since the crow, and he had answered every one of them politely, which Kit thought was probably worse for her than shouting.

"You should rest," Kit told him on the plank.

"I have rested for a week." He came down it carefully, one hand pressed flat over the stitches in his side, the shield left behind for once. Without it he looked less like a wall and more like a large tired man who had been pulled out of a river by his hair. "And you're going to do something foolish. I'd like to be close enough to watch."

*Gristle: is there a bear. tell me there's a bear*

*Silt: Let her be. She's earned a fair.*

There was no bear. Kit walked the meadow once, end to end, the way Maud had taught her: you don't set up where the crowd is, you set up where it's going to be. The puppet booth was too near. The geese were too loud. But between the chestnut drum and the ale tent there was a trodden patch where people stopped to eat and had nothing to look at, and a fiddler sitting on an upturned crate playing for nobody.

She bought a cup of chestnuts, ate half, and borrowed three tin cups off the ale tent and a dried pea off the chestnut girl.

"No magic?" Brannoc said.

"Not a mote." She turned a cup over on the crate beside the fiddler and smiled at him until he moved up. "I could do cups and balls before I could do my letters. It's honest work. Well. It's work."

*Gristle: BORING*

*Silt: Hush.*

And then, because a woman with a basket of eggs had slowed down to see what the girl with the cups was doing, Kit stopped listening to the scroll.

It was the oldest act she had. Three cups, one pea. *Here it is, and here it goes, and where it goes nobody knows.* The pea was under the first cup and then it wasn't; it was in the egg-woman's basket, in the ear of the egg-woman's boy, under the cup the boy swore blind was empty. People stopped. People always stopped for a child being fooled. Kit talked the way she'd talked at fifteen, in a voice a little bigger than her own and a little more Sinks, and let them catch her once, early, on purpose, so they'd think they had her measure. Then she took their pennies all afternoon.

The fiddler, whose name was Abel, caught on by the third round and began to play under her, a little run of notes each time a cup lifted. By the fourth he was playing her in. She borrowed his tune for a song she half remembered off a ballad-sheet, something about a miller's daughter and a drowned ring, and got the words wrong, and made the crowd tell her the right ones, which is the best way to get a crowd to sing.

She did not think about Corvin Thale. She did not count. Somewhere around the middle of the afternoon she noticed that she had not looked at the edge of her own sight for a long while, and the noticing was like missing a stair. She looked. The scroll was there, running, quieter than she was used to. She looked away again.

That was when she felt the first tithe.

It came in small. A mote, a warmth like a hand laid flat between her shoulder blades, and with it a name she had to reach for: *Thimble.* She'd named Thimble in her first year, for no reason she could now remember, and in seven years she didn't think it had said one word. Then another, *Oxbow*, and then *Wren's Well* and *the Tollhouse Saint* and a dozen others that had never been anything to her but numbers in a dawn count. None of them asked for anything. No request was pinned to the tithes, no *do a backflip*, no *make him bleed*. They just came, small and steady, while she lifted the cups and a boy shrieked with outrage at an empty one.

She had nothing to spend it on. She let it sit in her like sun.

*Silt: The quiet ones like this.*

*Silt: So do I.*

Kit took the pea out of the boy's own sleeve and the crowd roared, and over their heads she caught Brannoc's face.

He was leaning on a willow at the back with a cup of ale he hadn't drunk, and he was laughing. Not the short courteous breath he gave her jokes. Properly, with his head back, one hand clamped over his stitches because it hurt him and he couldn't stop. She had known him a month. She had seen him kill a ghoul and go into a river in his mail and sit through an hour of prayer to a god who never answered. She had never heard him make that sound.

She fumbled the next pass. Nobody noticed but Abel, who covered it with a run of notes, and Chat, who noticed everything.

*Gristle: oh no*

*Gristle: oh NO*

*Silt: Leave her be.*

She took the cups back to the ale tent at dusk with her apron pocket heavy and her face sore from grinning. Abel shook her hand and asked if she'd be at Harrowgate fair next month, and she told him she didn't know where she'd be next month, which was the most honest thing she'd said all day.

"You were happy," Brannoc said, on the walk back across the meadow. Lanterns were coming out along the bunting. Somebody had lit a fire for the chestnuts and the smoke lay flat over the grass.

"I'm always happy. It's my best feature."

"No," he said. "You're always *funny*. That's different."

She opened her mouth to have the last word and didn't have one.

---

The barge was dark but for Ysolde's lamp in the stern cabin. Kit sat out on the cabin roof with her knees up and her coat round them, under a sky full of cold stars, and counted.

She did it every dawn and she didn't usually do it at night, but the day had left her generous and she wanted to see them all: Thimble and Oxbow and the Tollhouse Saint, the ones she'd taken for furniture. She let the scroll slow and the names come up out of the dark the way she'd taught herself at fifteen, one at a time, a long roll-call down the edge of her sight. Four hundred and eighty-nine. Silt first, as always. Gristle, sulking about the bear. Lampwick, a steady small light. The lurkers, more of them lit tonight than she had ever seen.

And the grey ones. She had stopped looking at them closely in the last week because it did no good. A grey name was just a name with the colour gone out of it: watching, not speaking, not tithing. Ysolde had shown her how to tell them apart from the merely quiet, and Kit had wished she hadn't. One hundred and ninety-six of them now. She did the sum without wanting to. Forty-nine short of more than half.

Halfway down the roll was a name she knew better than her own.

*Pennywhistle.*

It was grey.

She looked at it for a long time, as if it might be a trick of the light, as if she'd look away and back and it would be the right colour again, and making some joke about her face. It had been a Warden for six years. It had been the first one to make her laugh in the Sump, on the fourth night, when she had thought she would never laugh again: a rhyme, of all things, a stupid skipping rhyme in the dark. And it had been quiet today. She had noticed, and been glad of the peace, and not asked.

"Penny," she said aloud. Her voice came out very small on the water. "Pennywhistle. You there?"

Nothing. The grey name sat in the scroll and watched her with her own eyes.

*Silt: Kit.*

*Silt: I didn't know. It didn't tell me. I would have told you.*

"When?"

*Silt: I don't know.*

"*When?*"

*Gristle: yesterday. the day before. what does it matter. it's sold*

Kit put the heels of her hands in her eyes, which did nothing, because they weren't watching through her eyes, not really; they were in her. They saw the dark behind her lids. They could see her doing this.

She thought of Corvin Thale's good manners on her doorstep, and his good bottle, and his voice saying *sustenance* like it was a kindness. She thought of him sitting somewhere tonight with Penny's name in a book.

So she did the only thing she could do. She reached for the hush.

It was a Warden's power, and hers: she could still a voice in the scroll for an hour, a day, a week. She'd used it on Gristle more times than she could count. She took hold of it now, and she did not choose. She swept it down the whole roll of grey names at once, every one of them, one hundred and ninety-six, Pennywhistle with the rest, and she put her whole weight on it, and said, "*Quiet.*"

The scroll went still.

It made no difference at all.

They hadn't been speaking. That was the whole of it. That was what grey meant. She had hushed a hundred and ninety-six things that had never said a word, and they sat exactly where they had sat, behind her eyes, looking out through them at the stars and the black water and her own knees, and whatever they saw, a man in Lanthorn could see too. The hush could stop a mouth. It couldn't close an eye.

She sat there until the cold got through her coat. Behind her the hatch creaked, and Brannoc's head and shoulders came up out of it, and he looked at her face and didn't ask, and came and sat down on the roof beside her, carefully, with his hand over his side.

*Gristle: kiss him or kill something. one or the other. i'm not fussy*

She laughed, because it was that or the other thing, and it came out wrong, and Brannoc put his coat round both of them without a word, and four hundred and eighty-nine of them watched him do it.
