At a quarter to midnight the coins were still coming.

Brannoc stood on the steps of the old chapter at the head of Iver's Square, where he had stood all night with the shield on his arm, and watched them come. They appeared on the step below him one at a time, out of nothing, with a small cold sound like a dropped key: pale coins, faceless both sides. Each one, Kit had told him, was a patron paying back its price. Each one was a name the square had said aloud tonight and would not now forget.

Corvin Thale sat on the step beside the heap with his pocket ledger open on his knee and his pen uncapped, and counted them. He had not written anything. He said the numbers under his breath, like a man telling beads. "Two hundred and three." A coin. "Two hundred and four."

The square was quieter now. Four hours of Kit's voice had worn the crowd down to the ones who meant to stay: Sinks women with sleeping children on their shoulders, buskers sitting on their cases, old men who had come to see the lamps lit and stayed for the rest. Osric was still on his ladder at the corner of Chandlers' Row, trimming a wick that did not need it. Ilse Marrable's wardens stood across every street into the square with their iron at their belts and their faces carefully blank. In the middle, where Kit had stopped them with their own names, the clerks still stood in a loose crowd, pens hanging from their fingers, listening to nothing.

Kit sat on the rim of the dry fountain with her knees up. She had no voice left. She raised a hand to him across the square and held up her fingers, closed them, opened them, and he understood: *none*. Not one grey name left.

A coin. "Two hundred and five," said Corvin, and capped his pen, and looked up at the bell tower over Chandlers' Row, and waited.

Brannoc did not understand why he had not written it down. He did not ask. He had learned on the Marle that with Thale there was usually a reason, and that it was usually worse than you'd think.

Then the bell began.

It was the Chandlers' bell, the one Kit had fallen from at the Midsummer fair the year before. On the first stroke the sky over the square went the grey of old paper. On the second the sealed door behind Brannoc's back, the chapter door, Saint Iver's door, with the House of Pale Coin's seal in white wax across its lock, began to speak.

It did not raise its voice. It never did.

"The books close on the twelfth stroke," said the door. "The factor will enter the position. The receipts of this evening are not to be entered. The sale instructed of the patron called Pennywhistle is to be entered as made. The position is two hundred and six."

Third stroke. Brannoc turned to look at Corvin, and Corvin was looking at the ledger on his knee, at the blank line waiting there, and he did not move.

"Further," said the door. "A pledged asset is located in the warlock's gallery."

Kit stood up on the fountain rim.

"It will be called," said the door, and on the fourth stroke it spoke again, and this time it did not speak in its own voice.

*Brannoc.*

He knew that voice. He had heard it once in his life, in a chapter at Wendmoor, the night the carts were in the yard. He had heard it in every hour of prayer since, by its absence, the way you hear the shape of a room by the one who isn't in it. It was low and warm and a little hoarse, like a man who has been up all night on a cold road, and it said his name the way nobody else had ever said it.

*Brannoc. I heard you. Every hour. I'm here. Only call me, and I'll answer.*

The shield was heavy on his arm. He found that his knees wanted to go. Three years and a half. Every hour. He opened his mouth and the name was right there behind his teeth, worn smooth with saying, *Saint*—

"*Don't.*"

It came across the square in a voice with nothing left in it, a cracked whisper that should not have carried and did. Kit, on the fountain rim, with both hands out as if she could stop him across forty yards of cobbles. Her face was wet.

"Brannoc. Don't call it. Please. Not to its face."

Fifth stroke.

And he saw it. Slowly, the way he saw everything, but he saw it whole. That she was not afraid for him. She was afraid for someone else. That she had been afraid for them a long time, quietly, on the towpath and at the banked fire and every night since Fennick when she lay with her back to him and didn't sleep. And that the voice behind him, which was his god's voice to the last breath of it, had said *it will be called*. A god did not speak of itself so. A ledger did, that had been taking down his prayers for three years and knew the sound of what they were addressed to.

*Only call me.*

He shut his mouth. He turned his back on the door. It was the hardest thing he had ever done, and he had once walked a fen at night with the causeway under water.

Sixth stroke. And out of Kit, out of her breast where she stood on the fountain rim, as if she had breathed it, came a light.

It was very small. No bigger than the flame of a wick. It hung a moment in front of her, and she put up her hand as if to cup it, and didn't, and let it go. It crossed the square, low, unhurried, over the heads of the sleeping children and the sitting buskers and the clerks, who turned their blank faces after it, and as it passed under each of Osric's lamps the lamp leaned toward it, and the light in it went a shade more gold.

He had seen that colour once before, at fifteen, across black water.

It came up the steps and stopped in front of him, at the height of his heart. He had not called it. It had come.

"The asset is located," said the door behind him, and Brannoc heard, for the first time, something in the dry voice that might have been satisfaction.

*No,* said the light.

Not to him. Past him, to the door. It was the same voice the door had used, low and warm and hoarse, and it was nothing like it at all.

*You hold my houses. Keep them. I am not in them. I am in this square, where every lamp was lit tonight by a man on a ladder whom nobody paid. You never held a lamp of mine. Nobody can. A god is where the light is kept.*

And it went into the shield.

He felt it: a warmth through the leather and the wood and the bones of his forearm, and then the lantern-housing in the boss, which he had trimmed every night for three years and a half and which had never once lit by itself, lit by itself. Not white, as at Saltcombe. Gold. A small, steady, ordinary gold, enough to read by, enough to see the next stone on a causeway.

*Brannoc,* said his god, from his own arm. *You kept the hours. I heard every one.*

Seventh stroke. He found he was on his knees after all, on the chapter steps with the shield across them, and couldn't say anything, and didn't need to.

"Saint Iver," he said anyway, eventually, to its face, because he could now. And it answered.

Eighth stroke.

"The factor will enter the position," said the door. No satisfaction in it now. "The factor's surety stands on the entry."

Corvin Thale uncapped his pen.

Brannoc watched him do it. He watched Thale look once across the square at Kit on the fountain rim, and once at the heap of pale coins on the step, and then down at the blank line. He wrote in a small, exact, clerkly hand, and Brannoc, kneeling at his elbow, read it as he wrote:

*Receipts this evening: two hundred and five, entered in full. Patron called Pennywhistle: no sale made. Position at the bell: nothing of four hundred and nine.*

He signed it. *C. Thale, factor.*

Ninth stroke. The page under his pen went grey, then darker, and words began to come up through it in a clerk's hand, the way they always had, and Corvin closed the book on them before they could finish.

"The tender is failed," said the door. "The factor's surety is called."

Corvin stood up. He looked, Brannoc thought, perfectly composed and about nineteen years old. He put the ledger in his pocket and straightened his cuffs.

Tenth stroke. Across the square Kit had gone very still, her head tilted, the way she went when the scroll was too loud to think. Then she laughed, cracked and helpless, and wiped her face with the heel of her hand and nodded, as if four hundred people had all said the same thing at once and she could only agree.

Something went through her. Brannoc saw it: she rocked on the fountain rim as if a wave had broken over her, and caught herself, and stood up straighter. She had told him once, on the towpath, what a tithe of fifty felt like, and what a hundred did. He had never seen her stagger.

"Corvin Thale," she said, in no voice at all, and the whole square heard her. "Your book. Last page."

Corvin looked at her. Then he took the ledger out again and opened it at the back, where his own contract was, and held it out flat on his two hands like a man holding a tray.

Kit put her hand out across forty yards of cobbles, palm up, the way she did when she was paying for a spell.

Eleventh stroke. The last page of the ledger filled with light, gold and silver and the green of river-weed and the grey-brown of oak-bark and colours Brannoc had no names for, four hundred and nine of them, poured in all at once. Writing came up through it, not in a clerk's hand. In a dozen hands, two dozen, crabbed and looping and childish and old, all at once and overlapping:

*Received of the gallery of Kit Tallow, in one tithe: the surety of Corvin Thale. The indenture of Maud Tallow. Paid in full.*

Twelfth stroke.

The sky over the square was black again, and full of ordinary stars. The door behind Brannoc was only a door. The white seal across its lock had cracked straight through.

Nobody in the square said anything for a long moment. Then, on his ladder at the corner of Chandlers' Row, Osric began, a little unsteadily, to applaud, and the Sinks women with the sleeping children joined him, and the buskers, and then everyone.

Corvin Thale sat down on the step, rather suddenly, beside the heap of pale coins, and put his face in his hands.

Brannoc got up off his knees. The shield was light on his arm, and lit. Across the square Kit was climbing down off the fountain, slowly, like someone who has been on a stage four hours and doesn't trust the floor. She looked up at him, and at the light on his arm, and her mouth made a shape he was too far away to read.

He thought it was probably *sorry*. He went down the steps to tell her there was no need.
