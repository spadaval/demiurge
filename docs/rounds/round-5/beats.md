1. s01-cistern: The Cistern
   Brannoc's eyes. Hired by the bridge wardens to clear the Marle Bridge cistern, he finds a young woman already down there, arguing with the dark: 'Twenty for a firebolt? It's a ghoul, not a dragon. Fine, thirty, but nobody gets to pick the hat.' She fights like a street act, bowing to nothing, spells flaring at odd moments as if paid for. Together they kill the ghoul. Brannoc stops to close the eyes of the last victim, a lamplighter. Kit flinches as if someone spoke. Climbing out, Brannoc stops on the ladder: 'Wait. Let me get this straight...' and the scene ends on her grin and 'Say hi to Chat.'
2. s02-lantern: Rules of the Window
   Kit's eyes, from here on mostly. At the Drowned Lantern she splits the bounty and explains Chat to Brannoc as a comic routine, reading the scroll aloud: Silt says he has kind eyes; Gristle wants to see him bleed; Pennywhistle wants to know if the shield comes off. Rules: the Window, tithes, requests, the Wardens. Why Chat? 'Old theatre word.' She doesn't say whose. He tells her about Iver's silence and the last vision. She mocks it gently, then less gently; she's nobody's lantern. She leaves him the bill. At home: 'Night, Chat.' Three hundred and twelve goodnights.
3. s03-grey: The Dawn Count
   Kit counts her gallery at dawn, the ritual she's kept since fifteen. The number is up but the voices are down. Dozens of grey names: watching, never tithing, never speaking. She jokes with Pennywhistle about lurkers. Pennywhistle doesn't laugh. A day of small busking in Chandlers' Square shows how she lives: every gesture timed for them, never a private face. Lampwick tithes a single mote when she gives a coin to a blind fiddler and doesn't play it up. At dusk, crossing Iver's Square, she passes an old man on a ladder lighting lamps nobody pays for; Lampwick tithes again, and she doesn't notice.
4. s04-stair: Tanners' Stair
   Ilse Marrable's wardens corner Kit on the stair: unlicensed pact, contrary to the Concordance. Kit tries to talk her way out as a show; Chat pays for smoke; the wardens have iron. Brannoc, who has been following her (oath-stubborn), walks up and offers her his arm: after dusk, the walker in the dark. Ilse knows the old right and honours it, warning Kit that the histories of Hollin the Many-Eyed are why the Concordance exists. A grey-haired woman with a cough watches from a doorway.
5. s05-treatise: On Choral Compacts
   Ysolde invites them up: rooms of banned books above a bookbinder's. She has waited thirty years to meet someone like Kit and interviews her like a specimen. She explains the Concordance, Hollin, and the law of majority holding. Kit goes white and counts: seventy-one grey names out of three hundred and twelve. Someone is buying. Ysolde says she can guess and won't say until she's sure. Brannoc notices she is lying about something.
6. s06-audit: The Factor
   Corvin's eyes. Late in the House of Pale Coin, he writes his acquisitions into the pocket ledger: seventy-one of three hundred and twelve. The ledger writes back in a clerk's hand: the Reckoning, the bell, his surety. We learn what he wants and what he is: courteous, tired, a man who keeps exact accounts of his own damnation. He thinks of Ysolde and doesn't write to her. He turns a pale coin over his knuckles. On a shelf, Maud Tallow's ledger page, which he has read.
7. s07-penny: Inside the Window
   Pennywhistle's eyes: what it is to be a small god in Kit's gallery. The Window as warmth, the taste of her bread, the scroll's endless noise; the dark behind it where the faded go. Pennywhistle remembers being sung on the Sinks school steps. A grey name sidles up in the whisper-dark and recites the Exchequer's terms: sustenance, for ever, no fading. Pennywhistle makes a joke of it and tells nobody, not even Silt. It counts how few children it heard skipping this year: none.
8. s08-weir: The Weir Wyrm
   Kit's answer to the buyer: grow faster than he can buy. She bills the eel-wyrm of the Sinks weir as a public show; half the Sinks turns out, Tobin in front. Brannoc warns her off the crowd. Chat pays big for a grander finish; she takes it; the weir-gate bursts and Tobin goes into the race. Brannoc gets him out, arm broken, head bleeding. The gallery leaps by nearly two hundred. Kit sits on the wet stones with the scroll roaring applause and can't hear any of it.
9. s09-door: A Gentleman Caller
   Corvin knocks at Kit's room over the chandlery with a good bottle and better manners. He doesn't hide what he is; it would be rude. He tells her Maud is alive and indentured in the Counting House, which Kit didn't know. His offer: sell the pact, Maud walks free, and Kit gets a quiet head, one patron who never talks. He lays down a pale coin: spend it at a crossroads at midnight when you're ready. Kit throws him out with a performance of scorn; when he's gone she sits with the coin and, for the first time, asks Chat to be quiet, and they aren't.
10. s09b-reave: Nan Reave's Rhyme
   The day after Corvin's call, Kit goes to the Reaves' room in the Sinks with all the weir money. Tobin, splinted, only wants to know if the wyrm is dead. Nan Reave takes the money, refuses the apology, and tells Kit the Sinks used to watch her shows for her, not for the dying. Then she sings Tobin to sleep with a skipping rhyme no one sings any more. In the scroll Pennywhistle, who has a joke for everything, has nothing.
11. s10-patience: The Barge Patience
   Kit, Brannoc and Ysolde go down the Marle on a coal barge toward Saltcombe and Hollin's testament, with Ysolde's money and Kit's guilt about Tobin. Gristle wants something to die; the bargemen think Kit talks to herself. Brannoc keeps the hours of prayer on the cabin roof at dawn and dusk; Kit notices Lampwick tithes during every one, a mote each, like a nod. She makes nothing of it out loud. Pennywhistle makes jokes; they're thinner.
12. s11-orrin: Orrin Bridge
   The toll-keeper at Orrin is a drowned man serving the Wet King. Kit plays the fight big, then at the height of a spell the grey names withhold together, as if at a signal, and the spell dies in her hands. Brannoc takes the drowned man's blow, goes into the river in his mail. Kit goes in after him with no magic at all, and gets him out by the hair. Gristle tithes for the first time ever for something that isn't blood: for her jumping.
13. s12-ninth: The Ninth Night
   In the barge cabin, Brannoc feverish, Kit keeps him awake with a story: how it started. The Sump, nine days dark, every play she knew by heart; Silt first, then a rhyme, then a tree; the river rising; the door bursting. Silt corrects her details on the scroll. Kit tells the bailiffs-took-Mum version, as she always does. Brannoc, half-asleep, asks why she was alone in the rooms when they came. She has never asked herself that, and changes the subject. 'Chat? It's what my mum called the cheap seats.'
14. s13-crow: The Crow
   Brannoc, mending, sees Ysolde give a letter to a crow at dawn. He confronts her with Kit there. She tells it plainly: the buyer is Corvin Thale, her student; he took the Exchequer's pact to pay off her debts, she found out too late, and she's here to get to him. The letter begs him to stop. Kit is furious and keeps her anyway: Ysolde knows the Exchequer and she knows Corvin. Brannoc says nothing for a day.
15. s14-sells: Sustenance
   Corvin's eyes. In a riverside inn upstream he reads Ysolde's letter twice and burns it. Then he goes into the whisper-dark of the gallery through the bought, and meets Pennywhistle. It is fading by the hour now: the newcomers are loud, nobody sings its rhyme. Corvin is gentle; he means it. Pennywhistle signs, and asks him not to tell Kit. He writes 196 in the ledger. The ledger writes: good.
16. s15-wending: Wending Fair
   A river-fair day. Kit busks without magic, cups and balls and a borrowed song, as she did at fifteen; she's good, and she's happy, and Brannoc sees it. The lurkers in the gallery, who never tithe, tithe for this, unasked, small and warm. Brannoc laughs out loud for the first time in the book. That night Kit counts and finds Pennywhistle grey. She calls to it; it doesn't answer. Hurt, she hushes all the grey names at once, which changes nothing: they still watch. She learns what Wardenship can't do.
17. s16-gristle: The Debtors' Oak
   Same night. The richest offer yet goes to Gristle, in the open scroll where everyone can read. Gristle answers with three names: the three men hanged from it for forged debts in 1089, and then the story of that morning, in its own voice, the only time it is ever not cruel. The gallery goes still. Kit, who never knew, tithes nothing and can only listen. Then Gristle says, as itself again: now kill something.
18. s17-saltcombe: Saltcombe at Low Water
   The drowned abbey shows itself at the ebb: salt cloisters, weed in the choir, and figures at desks still writing in the flooded scriptorium. Hollow clerks, Hollin's chorus, keeping books for a Throne three hundred years on. Ysolde is elated and terrified. Kit understands what the bought in her own gallery will become.
19. s18-testament: Hollin's Testament
   In a dry crypt above the tide line Kit reads Hollin's testament aloud, for Chat, a performer reading another performer's last lines. It is not the histories: Hollin closed her eyes to hide, starved her patrons, and they sold her for bread. Do not shut your eyes. Beside it, a roll of three hundred names, written so someone might remember. Ysolde wants the roll for scholarship; Kit takes it without knowing why.
20. s19-clerks: The Clerks Wake
   Corvin comes at the next low water with the Exchequer's writ, and the clerks rise from their desks. A fight in the flooding cloister. The grey names withhold; Kit is nearly helpless. Then Lampwick tithes, one patron, more than a hundred lesser powers could give, and Brannoc's lamp-shield lights white for the first time in three years. He stands in the gate and the clerks cannot pass the light. Ysolde, seeing Corvin's face, walks out to him and goes with him so it will stop. Corvin takes her. The tide comes in.
21. s20-curtain: The Curtain
   Night on the salt-flats. Every grey name sees what Kit sees; every plan she makes, Corvin reads. She has just read Hollin's warning, and she decides she has no choice: she closes the Window for one night to think in private with Brannoc. The scroll goes dark. Silence. She has not heard silence since she was fourteen. She falls apart; he stays. She doesn't know who she is with nobody watching, and finds out a little. She kisses him. Nobody sees.
22. s21-dark: The Gallery in the Dark
   Silt's eyes. The Window goes out and four hundred and eighty-nine starving small gods panic in the dark. Some flee; some fade where they stand. The buyers move among them with offers. Gristle threatens anyone who signs. Silt does what a fourteen-year-old once did in a vault: tells stories in the dark till morning, every one Kit ever told them, to keep them together. Lampwick holds the light of a single wick for the ones nearest. Silt sees it, and thinks she knows what it is.
23. s22-dawn: Dawn Count
   Kit opens the Window to a gallery thinned by eighty-seven. Two hundred bought out of four hundred and two: two short. Silt tells her what the night was. Kit understands Hollin from the inside, and swears out loud, for Chat, never to close it on them again. Chat asks what happened with the paladin. 'You'll never know.' It's the first secret she has had that is only hers.
24. s23-upriver: Against the Current
   Three weeks up the Marle by towpath. Between Kit and Brannoc now something Chat ships loudly and can't confirm. She teaches him to tell a story so a room listens; he teaches her to sit through an hour of prayer without filling it. In the third week he admits he has prayed three years into nothing and keeps doing it because stopping would be a lie too. Kit thinks about every hour of prayer and every single mote from Lampwick.
25. s24-ysolde: A Dying Woman's Offer
   Corvin's eyes, in the Counting House, a week after Saltcombe. Ysolde, under the grey paper sky, offers the Exchequer the rest of her life for Corvin's surety. It considers her for exactly as long as it takes to price her, and declines: a dying scholar against a multitude. Corvin learns, standing there, that she had known his price for a year and tried to pay it. On the way out they pass a clerk's desk where a woman with Kit's hands is copying figures and doesn't look up.
26. s25-lampwick: Lampwick
   A night camp on the towpath, Brannoc asleep. Kit whispers to Lampwick alone, the way she never talks to any one of them: what are you? It answers: Iver. It tells her about the foreclosure and why it cannot be named; it asks her not to tell him, because he would pray to it by name before he could stop himself, and the prayer would go to the mortgage. Kit lies awake beside the man she could give his god back to with one word, and doesn't.
27. s26-coin: The Pale Coin
   Lanthorn, a hard frost, eleven days to the Reckoning. Kit goes to Osric's lamps in Iver's Square for courage, then to the crossroads at midnight, Brannoc beside her. She spends the coin. Not to sell: to go in with the Window open, so every patron, bought or not, sees the inside of a Throne. Endless desks; a sky of paper; the scratch of ten thousand pens. Chat has never been so quiet.
28. s27-surety: Surety
   Kit finds Maud at a desk: grey, thin, twenty years older, still with a leading lady's way of looking up. Kit reads the ledger on the desk: Maud's debt, Maud's signature, and Kit's name pledged as surety a month before the night Maud ran. Maud gives her performance: she meant to come back. Kit recognizes every beat of it because Maud taught her them. The scroll goes wild; Gristle demands Kit leave her; Silt says nothing at all.
29. s28-ledger: The Ledger of the Many
   In the stacks Kit finds the ledger of her own bought patrons. Each contract binds a small god by its true name, and none of them remember theirs; that is why they sold. Pennywhistle's is there: the rhyme, the school steps, the year. Kit reads, and reads, a player learning a part in one hearing, while the Exchequer speaks to her through every clerk at once and makes its last offer, generous and patient. She understands what she wants now, and it isn't to keep them.
30. s29-surety-again: Surety Again
   The Exchequer's patience ends: the doors of the stacks begin to close. Corvin is at the last one with his hand on it. He does not close it. Maud, who has never paid for anything, sits down at Kit's side of the desk and signs herself surety for her daughter's exit, so the books will balance. 'Go on. I've had my bows.' Kit, who planned to save her, is saved by her, and hates it, and goes. Brannoc carries her the last stretch.
31. s30-count: Half
   Lanthorn, nine days out. Two hundred and five of four hundred and ten: exactly half, and one more sale ends it. Kit lays out the Naming to Brannoc and Ysolde's empty chair: tell the living who her patrons are, by their true names, on Lampnight, in Iver's Square. She needs a crowd, lamps, and no arrests. Osric will relight the square and find what's left of the Order; the buskers of the Sinks will bring the crowd; Nan Reave will teach the children the rhymes. Kit goes to Ilse Marrable and argues, with Hollin's roll, that the Swarm was a Throne's doing; Ilse doesn't agree to anything, and posts her wardens on every street into the square. Kit rehearses all week with the Window open, knowing Corvin is watching.
32. s31-listen: Penny on the Water
   Pennywhistle's eyes, grey. Its orders, in a clerk's hand: on Lampnight, bring one more patron over, the last sale, the one that makes majority; it is the friend everybody trusts. It watches Kit rehearse. On the fourth night, in Nan Reave's yard, it hears its own rhyme in an old woman's voice, children's feet slapping the stones, Tobin out of time with his arm in a sling. It finds, to its horror, it can remember its name.
33. s31b-eve: The Factor's Last Entry
   Corvin's eyes. The night before Lampnight, with Ysolde in the antechamber the Exchequer keeps for guests it may yet want. They talk as teacher and student for the first time in four years: the treatise, the debts, the price. She calls him an idiot for paying it; he calls her one for trying to buy him back. He writes his last entry in the pocket ledger: at the bell, the factor will register the tender as it stands. The ledger writes back nothing at all, which has never happened.
34. s32-lampnight: Lampnight
   Iver's Square at dusk: every lamp lit by hand, Osric and eleven grey paladins on ladders; a crowd of thousands, buskers, the Sinks, the curious; Ilse's wardens at every entry. Kit performs the show of her life with no spell at all: the stories of her patrons, each by its true name. Silt, the ford-goddess, whom the old river-wives still half remember. Lampwick's she skips. The crowd says the names back. One by one the grey names fill and buy themselves out; the bought count falls. Then the children come in skipping to Penny on the water, Tobin in front, and Pennywhistle, sent to make the last sale, buys itself back instead.
35. s33-clerks: Hollin's Names
   The Exchequer sends its clerks into the square to stop the show, hollow figures at a walk, pens still in their hands. Ilse's wardens hold the line and give ground. Gristle tithes everything it has for a fight, and Kit refuses it: no blood tonight. Instead she unrolls Hollin's roll and reads the clerks their own names, three hundred years forgotten. They stop. They stand in the lamplight and listen. Some of them weep salt.
36. s34-bell: The Closing Bell
   Brannoc's eyes. Midnight. The Exchequer, losing the tender, reaches for the one asset it can seize by name: the foreclosed god in the gallery. It calls Iver. Lampwick steps out of the scroll and into the square as Iver, a flame no bigger than a wick, and the deed has no hold on it: it stands in light kept by hand, not in a pledged house. Brannoc hears his god. The Exchequer turns to its factor: enter Pennywhistle's last sale as made at the bell, and majority stands on paper. Corvin, on the steps with his pocket ledger, enters the truth. Then the gallery, full for the first time in its life, pools what it has and pays Corvin's surety and Maud's indenture in one tithe. The tender closes failed; the bought count is nothing.
37. s35-after: Accounts Settled
   The morning after, on the square's cold steps. Maud walks out of a crossroads grey-haired and small, and Kit doesn't know what to do with her, and they sit together without performing, badly. Corvin and Ysolde, free, arguing about footnotes. Iver speaks to Brannoc once, kindly, and tells him it will not give him orders again: keep the light, if he wants, for those still walking. Then the gallery begins to go home, remembered: Silt to the old ford stones, Pennywhistle to the Sinks school steps, three hundred and forty-seven farewells in the scroll. Gristle stays: 'Who else would have me?'
38. s36-goodnight: Goodnight, Chat
   Two nights later, in her room over the chandlery. Sixty-three in the gallery. Kit tells them she's closing the Window for the night, not to hide, just because she wants to, and they say goodnight, and Gristle says something rude. She sleeps alone for the first time since she was fourteen. In the morning there's a knock: Brannoc on the step with bread. She opens the Window first. They're all still there. 'Morning, Chat.' She lets him in.
