The Lidless had no eyelids.

Tilly had known that, the way you know the words of a song. Rook had told her, and Casimir had told her, and Old Hob had told her at length. Knowing it was different from lying on her back on the high altar of the cathedral with her wrists and ankles strapped to the old iron rings and looking up into four of their faces. Their eyes were white to the edges and wet and wide open. The lids had been taken off long ago, so neatly that the skin had healed smooth. They never blinked. They didn't need to. Nothing they saw was theirs.

Above them, very far up, was the Eye.

It was lit. Every lamp in the cathedral had been turned toward it, hundreds of them in their brass cups along the galleries, and the great ground-glass lens at the crown of the dome gathered all that light and laid it on the altar in a single column, on her. It was warm, like lying in a bath of honey, and it was so bright that the rest of the cathedral had gone to shadow round its edges. Out there in the dark she could hear the Hierarch reading the decree. His voice was full and pleased, the voice of a man reading something he had written himself. *By the assent of the Concord. By shown cause. For the health of the city.* Beyond that, through the great doors, came the noise of Sallowcross pressed up the hill in its tens of thousands with lanterns in its hands. They were waiting to be told that the last of their old gods had been put down like dogs.

Somewhere above, in the dark of the dome gallery, Rook was crouched beside a brass lantern with its shutters closed. She couldn't see him. She hoped he couldn't see her.

*Steady*, said Mother Sluice, in the drift. *Steady, child. We're here.*

They were all there. Fourteen hundred and eight of them, the weight of them behind her eyes like a full house, so quiet that she could hear them breathing. Two hundred and sixty-one had stayed through the bad days, and the rest had come when she told the Sump. A grey glove hung among them. It had been silent for three weeks.

One of the Lidless drew a knife.

He did it without hurry, as a clerk opens a letter. He laid the blade along the inside of her left forearm and drew it down, and then the right. The cuts weren't deep, but they were long. She felt the blood run warm into her palms and drip off her fingers onto the stone.

*They want you to need it*, said Old Hob sharply. *Do you see? A coin to close a wound is a coin paid into the window. They want you to need power to live.*

"I see," Tilly said, through her teeth. The coins were already coming. Small ones by the hundred, from all over the drift, a canal-god's coolness, a hearth-god's warmth, Gnash's mean little sting. The cuts pulled at their edges, trying to close. And among the coppers, smooth and heavy, came one piece of gold that she hadn't asked for. Then another.

Then the Lidless began to break the jars.

They had them in a handcart, rows of them packed in straw, sealed with grey wax. Each one was the size of a preserving jar. They took them out one at a time, held them over her face in the column of light and broke them on the altar's edge.

It wasn't like being hit. It was like being drowned in other people's days.

The first jars were grey: corridors, ledgers, the backs of chairs in long rooms, the same staircase climbed a thousand times. They were the sight of clerks and wardens and officers who had sworn to the Velvet Eye and been skimmed of it a little every day for thirty years, until the rims of their eyes went white. It poured in through her eyes in a slow cold tide, and the window opened under it. She felt it open. Behind her, in the Gallery, she felt the cheap seats lean forward. Behind the cheap seats was the Hush, endless and grey, full of every small god in Sallowcross that had ever gone dim. The window had become a door, and the tide was pulling at it.

*Thirty-one in the hundred*, said Old Hob. His voice was very precise, which meant he was frightened. *Thirty-four. Thirty-six. The law counts it as paid in. Every drop. It counts it as the glove's.*

The next jar was bright.

She was standing on the Lowwater ferry stairs at dawn with a pole in her hands and the river coming up pink, and she knew every eddy by its name. That was Ansel Gorrie. Then she was at the top of a ladder on Lamp Row with a taper, and the wick catching, and the little flame steadying, forty years of it, every evening the same small satisfaction. Maud Pellin. Rope on the Weir Docks, rope burning through her palms, a man shouting a joke from a barge. Then the one stair of the old Sixth Canal that still showed above the rubble, where the pump-trough stood. A washerwoman's hands on a stone that had been worn into a hollow by centuries of other women's hands, and the hollow full of rainwater.

In the drift Mother Sluice made a sound like water going over a weir.

*My step*, she said. *That's my third step. It was always loose.*

Then the last jar from the top of the cart, which had a name on it in a clerk's tidy hand that Tilly couldn't read from where she lay. She didn't need to. It was dark, and a boy was waking up in a crate under the Lowmarket arches, warm and stiff, and a face was bending over him in the dark. He knew that face, and he was glad. He smiled at it. The face had a red ribbon in its hair.

Tilly made a noise she had never made in her life.

*Forty-four*, said Hob. *Forty-seven. Tilly. Forty-nine.*

And the drift went away.

Not gone, not like the hood. It was still there, but it was far off, behind glass, fourteen hundred voices she could see shouting and couldn't hear. In the quiet that was left there was only the column of light, and the cold tide in her eyes, and a grey glove.

*There*, said Greyhand.

It was kind. It had always been kind. Its voice was soft, and it smelled faintly of velvet and dust, like a box at the theater after the house has gone home.

*You're tired*, it said. *You've been tired since the Sump. You've done enough. It's very nearly mine anyway, you know; the law is the law. Give me the last of it willingly and I'll let you keep some of them. The little one. Pip. The old step and the canal. They'll be safe behind you in a corner, and you can talk to them, and nobody else will ever ask you for anything again.*

The cuts on her arms had stopped bleeding. She hadn't noticed when. Its gold had closed them.

*Nobody else will ever watch you again*, it said, very gently. *Isn't that what you've always wanted, under all the noise?*

Tilly lay in the warm light and thought about it honestly, because she owed herself that much. It was true that she was tired. And it was true, a little, under all the noise.

"You've watched me for six months," she said, out loud, so the Lidless could hear it too, "and you still think I'd sell the house."

*It isn't yours to sell*, said Greyhand. For the first time something under the kindness showed, like a hand under a glove.

"No," Tilly said. "That's my point. House up."

The glass broke.

The drift hit her like weather: fourteen hundred and eight voices, all shouting at once, and over all of them, louder than she had ever heard it, a ripple that flattened everything in its path.

*Everything*, said Mother Sluice. *Every one of you. Not coins. Not what you can spare. Everything you are, in the hat, now.*

They threw.

It came up through her like the Pit's Roar run backward, not out of her but into her, every small god in the house emptying itself at once. A gallows-god's rope, a well's cold, the smell of a forgotten bakery, a crossroads, a stair, a bell. It hurt in a way she had no word for. It was too much love, all of it given at once. The cuts on her arms split open again under the weight of it and closed and split, and the column of light shook.

*Forty-eight*, said Hob. His voice was cracking. *Forty-seven. It's falling. Keep on, keep on — no. Forty-eight. The jars, they're still breaking the jars —*

The Lidless hadn't stopped. A jar, and a jar, and a jar. The grey tide kept coming.

Gnash's crooked nail came up through the drift red-hot.

*Oh, for spite's sake*, said Gnash. *Nobody heckles her but me. Nobody.* And the nail poured itself out, every mean small scrap of power it had ever hoarded off her pratfalls in two years, and was suddenly a great deal smaller, and was still there.

Bellwether rang.

He didn't say *AGAIN*. He rang. Out in the cathedral, somewhere in the dark beyond the column of light, people heard a bell. It was a great bronze voice that hadn't sounded in two hundred years, the bell of Saint Aldo that had gone into the cannon foundry, ringing once from nowhere so that the stone hummed. The Hierarch stopped reading in mid-word. Somebody screamed. Bellwether rang again, and again, and every peal was everything he had.

Pip gave. It wasn't much, because Pip had never been much: a crack of light behind a cot, a child's whole attention for four years. He gave all of it, and for one terrible moment the crack of light at the edge of her sight went dark. Then it came back, very small.

*kept*, said Pip.

*Fifty*, said Old Hob.

And the ripple at the edge of her sight began to go flat.

*Child*, said Mother Sluice. She sounded quite calm now, and rather cross, as if she'd been interrupted in the middle of a wash. *When they let the water back into my canal — and they will, mind, you promised — you tell them the third step's loose. It always was. The women used to put a rag under it so it wouldn't rock, and they'll need to —*

The ripple went flat, and still, and then there was nothing where it had been. Tilly felt the count drop by one. Fourteen hundred and seven.

"Sluice," she said. "Sluice."

The house was empty. She could feel it, all of them spent at once, fourteen hundred gods lying in the cheap seats with nothing left. The grey tide kept on pouring in. And the glove hung there, soft and patient and heavy, exactly as heavy as all the rest of them put together.

*Fifty in the hundred*, said Old Hob, hoarse, into the silence. *Level. It hasn't passed half. Not yet. But there's nothing left to push it back with.*

Tilly lay in the column of light, bleeding again, and turned her eyes, which was all she could move. Somewhere at the far edge of the drift, past the spent sigils of fourteen hundred gods, past the glove, a ring of nine small bells hung where it always hung. It had thrown nothing, and it was watching. It always watched. It was turned toward the great doors.
