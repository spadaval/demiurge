She hadn't said *House up*.

That was the first thing Tilly understood, sitting on the bottom step of the vault with a blanket over her shoulders and the canal water still drying stiff in her hair. It was past eight. A grey square of daylight had come down the old sluice-shaft and lay on the brick floor in front of her, and the drift was dark. It had been dark since one in the morning, when she'd said *Curtain* and lain down on the sacking. All of them were sitting in the Gallery with their backs politely turned, waiting to be let in, while she counted.

She was good at counting. Rook had taught her: cards, coins, the house, the take. You count the house before you go on, so you know who you're playing to.

So she counted.

Ansel Gorrie, the fourteenth. She'd been asleep at Kettle Street; she had said Curtain. Maud Pellin, the seventeenth. Curtain. The twentieth, the night of the delving, she'd come home at one, shown Rook the leaf, untied her ribbon, said Curtain. And woken late and heavy-headed, as if she'd been carrying something all night, and there'd been canal mud on her boots, and she'd thought: the Underways. Of course the Underways.

Wick had been found at seven that morning, in the arches, a quarter-hour's walk from her bed.

The dockhand, the night after the stakeout: she'd slept the whole of the next night through, ten hours, like a stone, and been proud of it. Nell Brisk on the old canal steps, the night before the Wardens came. And then four nights in the hood, when she couldn't see and nothing could see through her, and nobody in Sallowcross had been hollowed at all. Casimir had told her that was Crane's proof. She'd laughed at it.

She wasn't laughing at it now. She sat with the numbers and they came out the same every time, the way a true count does.

Across the vault Casimir was sitting against the wall with his left arm across his knees. The sleeve was scorched to the elbow and the skin under it was red and shiny where the Roar had caught him. He'd taken the man from Cobb's Bridge to Bettany's in the dark and come back, and since then he hadn't said a word, and she was grateful for that. She'd never seen anyone be quiet so carefully.

"There was a thread," she said. Her voice came out rough. "Wasn't there. You went funny, after the Provost had you in. You borrowed my ribbon to look at under a glass and said it was for a cipher."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded.

"On a nail inside the boy's crate," he said. "At the height of someone kneeling. And Sump silt on your boots. I thought you were lying to me." He moved his burned arm and winced. "I thought you were doing it awake. I'm sorry. That was the wrong thing to be right about."

At the height of someone kneeling.

Wick asleep in his crate because his mother's room was full. Wick waking in the dark to see a face he knew bending over him. Of course he'd smiled. She'd sat by his bed in Saint Lenn's and done the vanishing queen for him with all the patter, and he'd smiled the whole time at a place just past her left ear. She had thought it was the hollowing that made him smile at nothing. It wasn't nothing. It was the last thing he'd seen: her own face, which he trusted.

She put her hands over her mouth and held them there until it passed. Then she took them away and wiped them on the blanket.

There was a way to do this. There was always a way. Rook's rule, which she'd had since she was eight: let them see you bleed, never let them see you bleeding. You come out, you show them the cut, you make it a bit, and the bit carries you through. *So, Chat, funny story.* She could hear exactly how it would go. She could hear the laugh she'd put in it.

She found she couldn't do it.

"House up," she said.

The drift came back the way blood comes back into a numb foot, all at once and hurting. *Morning*, they said. *Morning, Tilly. Morning. You're wet? Why's she wet. Where are we, still the canal. Who's he, oh it's him. Morning, Warden.* Old Hob's worn-step sigil, very proper: *Good morning. It is the tenth.* Bellwether's bell-mouth, sleepy, *morning morning MORNING*. Mother Sluice's ripple, slower than the rest: *Child. What's happened to his arm.*

Four hundred and two of them. She could feel the weight of them settle behind her eyes, and the gaps where the others had been before the hood, like missing teeth.

"I need you to listen," she said. "No Curtain. I'm not doing a bit. I need all of you to listen to this the first time, because I can't do it twice."

The drift slowed. She'd never asked them for that before. She'd always asked for *louder*.

So she told them. She didn't tell it well. She told them about Cobb's Bridge, waking in the black water with a man's eyelids under her thumbs, and his one white eye. She told them the dates, each of them, and what she'd been doing on each of those nights, which was sleeping, behind the Curtain, with all of them looking away. She told them about the thread on the nail at the height of someone kneeling. She told them about Wick smiling.

"It was me," she said. "My hands. Every one of them. I hollowed Wick." She heard her voice go up and pulled it back down. "I don't remember any of it, and that doesn't help anyone, so I'm not going to say it again."

For a heartbeat there was nothing in the drift at all, and she thought, absurdly, that they'd all gone, every one, at once.

Then it broke.

It came up past the edge of her sight faster than she could read it, glyphs climbing over glyphs. *No. No no. She's lying, she's sick, it's the hood. A sleeping window gets ridden, it's on the leaf. It's not her, it's one of us. Who? WHO. Someone doesn't keep the Curtain. I keep it. I always keep it. I never look, I swear on my well. Then who.* And under all of it, louder than the rest because it had been waiting for this its whole small spiteful life, Gnash's crooked nail:

*Told you. Didn't I tell you? Rotten all the way down. Pretty little conjurer. Rotten.*

"Yes," Tilly said. "All right. Yes."

*Don't you agree with Gnash*, someone wrote, appalled.

Patrons were going. She felt them go, and it wasn't like the hood, where they'd thinned slowly from hunger. It was quick and clean, a window shutting here and another there, small cold drafts behind her eyes. Most were the new ones, the strangers who'd come for the girl who fed a feral god a sunset, out of other cities and the far ends of the Hush. Some weren't new. A canal-god from the Brede she'd known since the Sump said *I'm sorry, I can't* and went. A little hearth-god said nothing at all and went. She didn't try to count them as they went. She knew she'd know the number at the end.

"Enough." Mother Sluice's ripple came up through the drift like a wave through weed, and it flattened everything. *Enough. Every one of you hear me.*

The drift went still.

*A sleeping window is ridden by the greatest share among the eyes left open*, Sluice said. *If the rest of us keep the Curtain, then the eyes left open are only the ones that don't keep it. So we'll have it said. Each of you. You'll stand up in front of her and say that you looked away. Hob, you'll write them down.*

*I have already begun*, said Old Hob.

They declared. It took a long time, and she made herself read every one. *I kept it. I kept it. I never looked, I'd never.* Bellwether, very small for him: *kept it. always kept it. sorry tilly.* Pip, a crack of light and one word: *kept.* Gnash, sourly, after everyone else had said it: *Course I kept it. You think I want to watch her sleep? Snores like a drain.* A ring of nine small bells shook once in the drift without a word. The bells-one didn't speak, and never had. Sluice let it stand.

At the end there was one sigil left that hadn't moved. It was a grey glove.

*Well?* said Mother Sluice.

The glove hung at the edge of Tilly's sight, soft and still, as it had always hung, the way it had hung over her in the Underways and in the hood when everyone else was starving. She found that she was holding her breath. Two nights ago it had told her, kindly, that it only wanted her safe.

*I can see I'm not welcome*, Greyhand said.

Then it said nothing more. It didn't leave; she'd have felt that, a window the size of a church door shutting. It just went quiet, the way it had been quiet for five months before it ever spoke, and the glove went on hanging at the edge of her sight.

The drift waited for her to say something.

She couldn't think of one thing to say. She sat on the bottom step with the blanket round her and felt the count settle, the way a house settles after the doors close. Two hundred and sixty-one. A hundred and forty-one of them gone in the time it takes to tell a story badly.

Casimir got up off the floor. He crossed the vault and sat down on the step beside her, not touching her, his burned arm held carefully on his knee. He didn't tell her it wasn't her fault. He didn't tell her anything. After a while she leaned, very slightly, until her shoulder was against his, and he let it stay there.

*Tonight*, said Mother Sluice, much quieter, *you'll sleep with the house up.*

Tilly laughed, which hurt. "You won't want to watch that. Gnash is right. I snore."

*We'll watch in shifts*, Sluice said, as if she hadn't spoken. *Hob will make a roster. Two hundred and sixty-one pairs of eyes open on you, and whatever wants to ride you will have to get past all of us first.* A pause, and the ripple went still. *Somebody should have been watching you all along, child. That's on us as much as you.*

*I volunteer for the first shift*, Old Hob said. *I am a step. I am accustomed to being stood on in the night.*

*AGAIN*, said Bellwether, very softly, which from him meant something else entirely.

Tilly stood up. The blanket slid off her shoulders. She said, "I need a minute," and heard how that sounded, because there was no such thing as a minute. There was Curtain, or there wasn't. She didn't say it. She walked down the long vault toward the old lime-kiln at the far end, where the brick was black and the daylight didn't reach, with two hundred and sixty-one gods watching her go and one grey glove among them.
