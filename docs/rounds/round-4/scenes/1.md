The Provost kept a lamp burning on her desk at three in the afternoon, with the sun full on Marrowgate Square outside, and nobody in the Warden-House ever mentioned it.

Casimir stood on the worn place in the carpet where junior wardens stood and waited to be noticed. He had been summoned by a runner with no reason given, and he had spent the walk up three flights of stairs going through what she might have a reason for. He'd been out of the House five days in six. He had filed nothing since the bell-tower, because there was nothing he could file. His reports said *inquiries continuing*, and that was true, if you didn't ask who he was inquiring with.

Crane finished the line she was writing, sanded it and set the pen in its tray. Then she looked up. In the lamplight the milk at the rims of her irises looked almost like light, a pale ring round the grey, the way it went on all of Vessary's long-sworn. He had been told once by an older clerk not to stare at it. He looked at her forehead instead.

"Lorne. Close the door."

He closed it.

She opened a drawer, took out a folded square of evidence-paper and laid it on her blotter, squarely in the pool of the lamp. She unfolded it with two fingertips, one corner and then the next, the way you'd open a letter you already knew the contents of.

On the white paper lay a thread. It was a single strand of silk, a little longer than his thumb, frayed at one end where it had parted. It was red, and twisted through the red was a filament of gold that caught the lamp and threw it back.

"Sergeant Abery went back through the Lowmarket arches on the second day," Crane said. "Everyone else had stopped looking at the crate the boy was found by, because the boy was what mattered. Abery is not a clever man, but he's thorough. This was on a nail on the crate's inside edge, at about the height of a person's head if they were kneeling."

Casimir looked at it. He made himself keep looking at it. The silk had been on his mind for five days without his knowing it, which was the worst way for a thing to be on your mind: he'd seen it at the edge of every conversation on the Kettle Street roof, bobbing as she talked, its tail end caught in her collar when she turned her head to listen to something he couldn't hear. He'd watched her tie it the morning after the Sump with the end in her teeth.

"Silk," he said, because she was waiting.

"Silk, and not new. I had it to Merrin's on Draper Row." Crane touched the gold filament with the end of her pen, not quite touching. "Old Vessen stage-silk, he says. The gold is wound on a linen core so it'll take hard wear under footlights. Nobody's imported it in forty years. A dressmaker wouldn't have it. A theater might, in a costume trunk, if the theater was old enough."

"There are a good many old theaters in the Lowmarket, Provost."

"There are eleven," Crane said. "I had them counted." She sat back. The chair creaked. "Have you seen her again? The girl from Tannery Yard. The one with the three hundred."

The question came without weight, the same as the others, as if it were the next line in a ledger. That was how she asked everything, and it was why people answered her.

Across the square the cathedral clock began on the hour, and inside his coat the watch answered it, nine small bells struck so close together they were nearly one sound. Crane's eyes moved to his coat and back. Everybody in the House knew what the watch was, and whose. Some of the older clerks found it touching that the Abbess's warlocks still carried them. Some didn't.

Casimir let the bells finish. He was aware, the way you're aware of your own tongue, of the place behind his eyes where his patron sat. She sat there now, as she always did, and saw what he saw: the thread, the lamp, the milk-rimmed eyes across the desk. If she had a view on any of it she kept it, as she kept everything.

"Not yet, Provost," he said.

He'd meant to say *no*. *Not yet* had come out instead, which was worse, because it was a promise as well as a lie.

Crane nodded slowly, as if that were the answer she'd expected, and for a moment he thought she was going to dismiss him. Instead she turned her chair a little toward the window. Below it Marrowgate Square was full of afternoon: a knife-grinder's wheel, the barracks gate standing open, two cadets in shirt-sleeves carrying a bench across the cobbles.

"Do you know why the House is on this square, Lorne?"

"The old lane was here. Marrowgate Lane. They cleared it and built the barracks."

"They cleared what was left of it," she said. "I was ten. We lived on Cutler's Steps, which is gone now too, and my sister Hester was sixteen and had a half-day from the bindery, and she went to the night-market in the lane because there was a man there who sold paper lanterns shaped like fish. She wanted one for me. It was my birthday the week after." Crane's voice didn't change. It was the voice she used for duty rosters. "I was put to bed. I woke up because the sky was the wrong colour."

Casimir said nothing. He had heard of the Marrowgate burning since he was a boy in the Wardens' school; it was in the primer. He had never heard her say her sister's name. He hadn't known she had one.

"You know what they teach. The well-god had been starving a hundred and fifty years by then. It went mad with it, the way they do, and it got into the crowd and rode them. It wanted eyes, any eyes. People walked into the well with their own eyes open, smiling, because it wanted to see the water from underneath. Vessary's warlocks came and sealed the lane at both ends so it couldn't ride anyone else out into the city, and they burned it out. Thirty-one dead." She said the number as if she'd counted them herself. Perhaps she had. "They found Hester at the east end, by the barrier. They said she must have been trying to get out, and the god wouldn't let her go. They gave my mother a shoe."

The lamp hissed softly. Outside the cadets had got the bench through the gate.

"I was sworn to the Velvet Eye at nineteen," Crane said, "because its warlocks were the ones who stood in that fire. And every year since, somebody has come into this office and told me the small gods are pitiable. That they were done an injustice. That they only want to see. Some of them have been very eloquent." She turned back from the window, and the milky rims caught the lamp again. "I don't doubt they're pitiable. A starving dog is pitiable. You still don't let it near the children."

Something in his face must have moved, because she looked at him a moment longer than she needed to.

"Amberley's mother came here yesterday," she said. "She stood where you're standing. She wanted to know whether her son would see again. I told her the truth, which is that no one has ever seen again." She folded the evidence-paper back over the thread, one corner and then the next. "Three in a fortnight, Lorne. Through a window with many patrons. You have seen the ward at Saint Lenn's: you know what that means as well as I do. There is one warlock in this city with many patrons that we know of, and she was out under the Lowmarket on the night the boy was taken, and she was seen with a red ribbon in her hair."

*Seen*, he thought. *By me.* He had put the ribbon in his first report himself, a line of description, because a careful man describes what he sees and he had not yet known there would be a reason to be careless. He'd forgotten that line until this moment.

"The Synod wants cause," Crane said. "I would rather give it to them than give Amberley's mother another reason to come up my stairs. When you find her, you hood her. You don't talk to her. They're good at talking, the ones with small gods behind them. They've had a long time in the dark to practise." She slid the paper back into its drawer and closed it. "That's all."

"Provost."

He went out and shut the door softly behind him, as the House taught. The corridor was cool and smelled of floor-wax. Halfway down it there was a window onto the square, and he stopped at it without deciding to and stood with his hands flat on the sill.

Eleven old theaters. Crane didn't guess; she counted. In a day or a week Abery or some other thorough man would knock on the door of the twelfth, which nobody had counted because its sign had fallen down years ago, and an old drunk would open it, and behind him on a hook would be a costume trunk full of Vessen silk.

Casimir thought of the boy in the long ward, smiling at the place beside your head. He thought of a dead well-god that wanted to see water from underneath. And then of Tilly on the roof four nights ago, describing to him in five different voices what three hundred gods remembered of a city before the Synod made it tidy. She'd stopped in the middle of one of the voices, gone quiet as if listening, and said, *Sluice says you look like a man who's never been told a story he didn't have to check.*

He had checked everything his whole life. He'd never in his life told the Provost anything that wasn't true.

He took the watch out of his coat and looked at it, though he knew what time it was. The case was warm from his body. It said nothing. It never said anything, and it struck nine bells every hour regardless, like someone clearing her throat in the next room and never coming in.

"Did I do right?" he asked it, under his breath, in the empty corridor.

The hands moved on. He put the watch away and went down the stairs, past the duty desk and the evidence room where a thread lay folded in a drawer. He went out into Marrowgate Square, where they had cleared what was left. He told himself he was going to the twelfth theater to look at a ribbon. He was not quite sure, yet, whether he was going there to take it as evidence or to tell her to burn it.
