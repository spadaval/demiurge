She had been sitting behind the head of Wick's bed since three in the morning, where he wouldn't see her.

The long ward of Saint Lenn's was grey with the beginning of light. The nuns had been up all night. They'd gone from bed to bed with their lamps when the sight came home, a little before ten, while the bells were still going all over the city. The ferryman had sat bolt upright and shouted for his pole. The lamplighter had wept. Jory Tamsett had looked at his own hands for an hour. Nell Brisk had asked for her washing. But Wick had slept straight through it, the way he slept through everything, on his back with his mouth open. Sister Agathe had looked at Tilly's bandaged arms and the state of her face and said, "You'll want to be here when he wakes," and brought her a stool and gone away.

So she'd put the stool behind the bedhead, against the wall, where his eyes wouldn't land when they opened. The last thing those eyes had seen was her face bending down over a crate in the dark. She wasn't going to be the first thing as well.

*He's breathing different*, said Old Hob.

The drift was quiet this morning. It had been quiet since the cathedral. There were fourteen hundred and six of them, and she could feel them lying all through the cheap seats like a house after a long show, too tired to go home. Some of them were already looking toward home all the same, toward bridges and wells and street corners where people had stood last night with lanterns in their hands and looked straight at them for the first time in two hundred years. Gnash was a small mean spark. Pip was a crack of light the width of a hair. Bellwether was hoarse, and he hadn't said *AGAIN* once.

And she kept looking for the ripple. All night she'd caught herself doing it, turning her inner eye to the place in the drift where Mother Sluice always was, slow and cross and steady under everything. There was nothing there. It was like reaching for a banister that's been taken down.

*Tilly*, said Hob gently. *The boy.*

Wick sighed, the long cross sigh of someone who doesn't want to get up, and opened his eyes.

They weren't white. They were brown, ordinary muddy brown, with the grey light from the high windows in them. They wandered over the ceiling and didn't understand it and came down. On his pillow, propped against the wall where the nuns had left it for a month because he'd held it in his sleep, was a worn playing card: a queen in a red gown with a lamp in her raised hand.

Wick looked at it for a long time.

"That's the queen of lamps," he said. His voice was rusty. "That's Tilly's."

*Oh*, said the drift, all fourteen hundred of them at once, a single soft breath.

Tilly found she couldn't get up. She sat on the stool with her bandaged hands pressed flat between her knees and listened to him reach for the card and drop it and swear and reach again.

"I had a dream," he told the ceiling. "I had a dream Tilly came down the arches in the night. Only it wasn't —" He stopped. He'd got the card. "Tilly?"

"I'm here."

He turned his head too fast and winced, and found her, and his whole face went bright the way it always had. She made herself stay where she was and be looked at.

"It was me," she said. "In the arches. It was my hands. It wasn't me driving them, but they were mine, and you smiled at me, and I'm going to tell you all of it, every bit, when you can stand to hear it. I'm not going to make it a story. I'm just going to tell you."

Wick considered this with the deep seriousness of someone who has just woken up.

"All right," he said. "Can you do the queen first?"

She laughed, and it came out wet. "You do it. Your hands work now. Go on."

He fumbled it. He fumbled it twice, dropped the card in his blanket, called it a name the nuns would have washed out of his mouth, palmed it on the third go, badly, with his thumb showing, and held up his empty other hand to her with a grin so wide it looked like it hurt. The drift went off like a box of fireworks. Gnash, small as a spark, threw him a coin for the thumb.

---

Casimir came in at half past seven with the Provost on his arm.

Tilly knew her by her walk before she saw her face: straight-backed, chin up, putting each foot down as if the floor were a report she distrusted. She'd been led before by no one. Casimir was guiding her with two fingers under her elbow, the way you guide someone through a crowd. He was still in last night's coat, with the scorched sleeve and the Wardens' badge back on the breast of it, and he looked as if he hadn't slept since the Sump.

Crane's eyes were white now, all the way across. There was no grey left in them, no rim of milk. They were white like the Lidless's, like the hollowed people's had been, and they moved toward voices a little late.

"Brask," Crane said. Casimir must have told her where to look. "You're there."

"I'm here."

"Lorne says the boy is awake."

"He's doing a card trick," said Tilly. "Badly."

Wick, who had gone very still at the sight of the Warden's coat, did the trick again, just as badly, out of sheer defiance.

Crane stood by the end of the bed. For a moment she didn't say anything, and her face didn't do anything, and Tilly thought of the duty-roster voice Casimir had told her about. Then Crane said, "Tell me what he looks like."

Tilly looked at her.

"I can't, now," said Crane. "My patron took what it had left of me last night. It was its right; I'd sworn it. I stood over every one of these people and told myself a starving god had done it, and I'd have starved every god in the Hush to make it right. I would like to know what they look like. You have the eyes for it. You have more eyes than anyone in this city."

So Tilly told her. She told her about the boy's brown eyes with the light in them, the hair sticking up at the back where he'd slept on it for six weeks, the card in his fist with the thumb showing, the grin. She did it plainly, no patter. In the drift fourteen hundred small gods listened to her describe a boy to the woman who had wanted them all starved, and none of them said a word.

When she'd finished, Crane nodded once.

"I hooded you," she said.

"Yes."

"On that evidence I'd do it again." Crane's white eyes rested somewhere past Tilly's shoulder. "That's the part I'll be saying at the inquiry, if they let me. Not that I was deceived. That a sound procedure, done honestly, by someone who meant well, put a girl in a sack for four days and very nearly fed the whole of the Hush to the thing that wrote the procedure." She drew a breath. "The Synod sat all night. The Concord's licences are suspended pending inquiry, all five Boxholders, even Lorne's. The Hierarch will have resigned by noon; he doesn't know it yet, but the Synod does. And the Wardens' charter goes back to the drafting table, which is where it should have been two hundred years ago."

"What happens to you?" Tilly said.

"I give evidence." Crane turned her head toward Casimir's hand on her arm. "Lorne. Take me to the ferryman. I'd like to hear him talk about his river."

---

Afterwards Tilly found Casimir on the hospice steps, sitting in the early sun with his burned arm across his knees, watching the city wake up. Sallowcross looked as if it had been at a very long party. There were lantern-stubs on every step and bridge rail, and people standing about in twos and threes talking in low voices and looking at the oddest things: a well-head, a stair, the corner of a building where there had been nothing to look at for two hundred years.

She sat down beside him.

"She spoke to me," Casimir said.

He didn't look at her. He was looking at the dead tower of Saint Aldo, black against the morning over the Lowmarket roofs.

"I went up the tower after. About four. I couldn't think where else." He turned the nine-bell watch over in his good hand, not opening it. "And she spoke. To me. Not through you, not through the drift. Just — her. One sentence."

Tilly felt the drift come up off the floor all at once like a flock of birds. *What did she say. WHAT did she SAY. Ask him. Tilly ask him. Did it hurt? Did it burn? What did she —*

"Did it burn?" Tilly asked.

"No." He sounded surprised, still, hours later. "I was braced for it my whole life. It didn't. It was —" He stopped and thought about it with great care, the way he thought about everything. "It was the right size."

*THE SENTENCE*, said Bellwether, hoarse.

"Chat would like to know," Tilly said, "what the sentence was."

Casimir smiled. It was small and private and didn't belong to anybody else. She had never seen it before, in six weeks of looking at him.

"I know they would," he said. "It's mine."

The drift howled. Gnash called him a miser. Pip said *fair* very quietly. Somewhere at the far edge of it a ring of nine small bells, which was still there and seemed to mean to stay, shook once, and went still, and said nothing at all.

Tilly put her shoulder against his. He let it stay.

*Good morning*, said Old Hob, a little late, because no one else was going to say it and someone always had. *It is the second.*

She waited for the ripple to correct him on something, the way it always had.

It didn't. She sat on the step in the sun and let the quiet be there.
