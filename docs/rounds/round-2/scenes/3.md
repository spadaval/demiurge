The shepherd's hut had one window, no glass, and a sack nailed over it that the wind kept lifting like someone trying to look in. Bry had stopped hearing it. She sat with her back against the wall by Hal's pallet with the tinderbox open on her knee, and every hour or so she leaned over and put her hand on Hal's forehead, and every hour it was a little less like touching a kettle.

"You're doing it again," Hal said, without opening his eyes.

"Doing what?"

"Checking. I'm not dead. I'll tell you when I'm dead. I'll be very clear."

"You were singing hymns at me yesterday. Backwards."

"That was liturgy." He winced and shifted his bandaged side. "Quillon liked it backwards. Ask him."

She asked. Quillon was a thread of grey-brown at the very edge of the scroll, so faint she had to squint, and what he said was *he oiled it wrong again*, which meant nothing to her and made Hal laugh until he coughed.

Thimble was a small orange flame curled in the bottom of the tinderbox, singed black at the edges where the brass bottle had scorched it. It had been back five days and it had not stopped working once. Every few breaths a voice in the scroll went grey and still, hushed.

*Thimble: and you can stay quiet too*
*just a friend: but friend*
*Thimble: you're not my friend*

Eight hundred and something of them now, Thimble said, out of seventeen hundred and something. Nearly half the sparks that came down in the hut came down brass, and Bry could feel each one land and sink in and sit in her like a coin in a shut fist. She didn't know how to stop them. She had sat up three nights now with the window open, listening, and she still didn't know.

The sack over the window lifted. Snow came in. And with it, out of the dark corner where the firelight didn't go, a long shadow stood up from the floor and became a man.

Bry had her hand up before she knew it, and fire in it.

*KILL HIM*
*Gorehound: KILL THE CROW KILL THE CROW*
*kill him, friend!*
*burn him*
*KILL HIM*
*Thimble: HUSH*

She didn't need a vote for this much. That was the one gift the brass had given her that she still liked. The flame stood in her palm the size of an apple, white at its heart, and Corvin stood in the corner with snow on his shoulders and his hands open and empty at his sides and didn't move.

"I'll go if you tell me to," he said. "I'd rather say something first."

"You said plenty. Sixteen days ago. I remember it all."

"This is something else."

Hal had pushed himself up onto one elbow. He looked from one of them to the other and said, in a voice that was nearly his own, "Child, the snow's coming in. Either burn him or shut that sack."

Bry looked at the flame. Then she closed her hand on it and it went out, and the hut was darker, and she got up and shoved the sack back into the window frame and stuffed the gap with straw. When she turned round Corvin was crouched by the fire, holding his hands to it. They were raw red with cold. There were five white beads at his throat, not four. She noticed that and filed it away to hate later.

"You've been home, then," she said. "Under your hill."

"Yes."

"Did she pat your head?"

"She gave me tea." He looked into the fire. "I didn't drink it."

She sat back down by Hal, as far from him as the hut allowed, which was not far. The scroll was calming. Thimble had hushed Gorehound and a good forty of the brass, and the rest of them were murmuring *crowboy* and *he's back* and *ooh*, the way they did. The silver was gone.

She noticed that too.

"Go on, then," she said. "Something else."

He was quiet for a while. When he spoke it was in the flat, careful voice he'd used on the road to Hob's Crossing, explaining the rules of his pact as if they were the rules of someone else's.

"There's a cage in her hall. Silver wire. Big enough to stand in, if you don't mind standing. I told you your mother sang for her. That's where she sang. For eleven years. I'd hear it whenever I came in to make my report. The Queen likes to be sung to while she listens to other things."

Bry said nothing. Her hands were in fists in her lap.

"It's empty," Corvin said.

"Empty."

"I asked. She told me, as if it were a story about a cat that got out. Mortals under the Hill thin, if they're kept long enough. They forget themselves a piece at a time, and what's left gets small and light and fey. She said your mother thinned and flew. Not this spring gone; the one before. A year and a half ago. Slipped through the wires one night and out through a mirror. She said it was a pity. She said your mother had the loveliest voice she ever lent."

"Flew," said Bry.

"That was her word." He turned one of his hands over in the firelight, as if there were something written on it. "I asked what she'd become. She said she hadn't stayed to look. Something pale. With wings."

Bry looked down at the scroll.

There was no silver in it. There hadn't been since the shadow stood up in the corner. There never was, she thought, slowly. There never was, when he came in. In the Gull, the night he walked in with the writ: one moment a pale line asking whether she'd had her supper, and then he was in the doorway, and nothing. On the road. In Crake. She had thought it was shyness, because Mothlight was new and odd and asked odd questions. Some of them did go quiet around strangers.

The crow-man took the singer of Lessering into the Hill. I saw.

*Have you eaten today?*

*That's a great many new people. Are you sure?*

*Get her off that gate.*

*Come away from the water, love. You're shaking.*

Her heart had begun to go very hard and very slowly, the way it had gone on the chandler's roof the night she held the writ up next to her mother's note and saw the same green wax.

"When," she said. "When did she get out? Exactly."

"The Queen said the spring of last year. Late spring. The hawthorn was out."

Mothlight had come in June. A summer evening, Bry remembered, because she had been singing on the quay at Lessering for the boatmen and a new line had come up in the scroll in a colour she'd never seen, pale as the inside of a shell, and asked her what the song was called. She'd told it. The new voice had said: *I know that one.* And Bry had laughed and said everyone in Lessering knew that one, and written in the guestbook that night, *Mothlight. Silver. Asks a lot of questions.*

She didn't say any of that aloud. She was afraid to. If she said it out loud in front of seventeen hundred watchers, and it was wrong, she didn't think she could bear to hear them be kind about it.

"Bry," said Hal quietly.

"I'm all right."

"You're not."

"No," she agreed.

Corvin was watching her face. He wasn't a stupid man. She thought he had probably got there before she had, somewhere on the road back, walking through the snow with five white beads at his throat; she thought he had probably been carrying it the whole way, not knowing if he had the right to say it. He didn't say it now. He only said:

"There's more. You should have all of it."

"Then give me all of it."

"She knew whose daughter you were. When she sent me. She knew before the writ was sealed. She sent me because I'd been the one to fetch your mother, and she thought that was elegant." His mouth moved, not quite a smile. "She asked me once, on the road, whether you sang. I told her no."

"I sing all the time."

"I know."

"Why did you lie to her?"

"Because she wants you in the cage," he said. "Not unmade. That's what she'll ask the Court for, out loud: that the pact be broken. So you're nothing but a girl with a pretty voice. And then she'll ask for the girl. A thousand voices in one throat; that's how she put it. She has a hole in her collection and she means to fill it with you."

The fire cracked. Behind her, Hal breathed in and out, slow. In the scroll, Thimble's flame-orange came up very small: *bry*.

"So I'm not taking you to her," Corvin said. "I'll take you to the Court, because you have to go, and Dov needs you to, and so does he." He tipped his head at Hal. "But I won't give you to her. Whatever else happens. That's all I came to say."

"And your name?"

"She can keep it."

Bry looked at him for a long time. Under the soot and the cold he looked older than he had in the Gull, older than thirty-one. The thorn beads clicked when he breathed.

"I'm not going to forgive you," she said.

"I didn't ask you to."

"You took my mother. You put her in a birdcage and you listened to her sing in it for eleven years while you made your reports. I was in the garret counting drips."

"Yes."

"You don't get to make that right by standing in a doorway for me."

"No," he said. "I know. I'm going to stand there anyway."

She found that she had nothing to say to that, so she didn't say anything. The scroll was saying things: *aww*, and *crowboy*, and *forgive him!!*, and some of the brass wrote *kill him, friend* again, lazily, as if out of habit, and Thimble hushed them. Hal had lain back down and closed his eyes, and his mouth was moving, praying or arguing, or both; with Hal and Quillon there was never much difference.

At last she got up, took the heel of the loaf off the shelf, stuck it on the end of her knife and held it in the fire until it caught and blackened round the edges. She did it without thinking. She always burned it. She knocked the flame off on the hearthstone, tore it in half, and held one half out across the fire.

Corvin looked at it.

"Eat it or don't," she said. "That side's yours. Of the fire. Not this side."

He took it. He ate it, burnt edges and all, sitting on his heels on the far side of the hearth with the snow melting off his coat.

Bry sat down again by Hal and pulled the blanket round her shoulders, and looked at the scroll, at the very bottom of it, where a single pale spark had come down while she wasn't watching and was still sinking, slow as a moth, into the back of her hand.

There was nothing written beside it. Just the spark. Silver.

She closed her hand over it and kept it there all night.
