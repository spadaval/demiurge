Bry woke because her teeth hurt.

That was how the count always came to her first, before she opened her eyes: a pressure in the jaw, as if she had been grinding them all night on a number. Three hundred and one had been a dull ache, like a tooth going bad. This was something else. This was a choir of tuning forks.

She opened her eyes.

The scroll was a waterfall. It came up the left edge of her sight so fast the words smeared into a bright ribbon, and through the shutters of the Brindle's back room the morning was grey and wet and ordinary, and none of that mattered, because the number sitting at the bottom of the scroll, where it always sat, the way a cat sits on the end of a bed, said 915.

"No," Bry said aloud.

Corvin was already downstairs, at the table by the kitchen hatch, eating porridge like a man performing a solemn rite in which porridge had a part. Hal was opposite him, reading his codex with a spoon in his fist and his spectacles at the end of his nose.

The Wet Gentlemen scrolled away before they finished, carried off by the brass. Bry had never seen that happen before. Usually the scroll waited for the Gentlemen out of sheer politeness, because there were nine of them and they took a while.

Corvin put his spoon down. He looked at her, and then very briefly at the air beside her left ear, where she was always looking, as though he might see something there if he were careful enough.

"Tallow's," he said.

"Mr. Tallow's," Bry agreed. "His principal's. Whoever. They came. Hal, it's more than double. I've never been past four hundred and thirty and I only got there because you threw me at a wall."

"You threw yourself at the wall," said Corvin. "I declined to catch you."

"Very gallant." She sat down on the bench next to Hal and stole his bread. Her hands were shaking. She held one up flat in front of her, palm down, and thought, idly, *up*, and the salt cellar on the far side of the table rose off the wood and stayed there, turning slowly, like a small planet considering its options.

She had never been able to do that without a vote. Never. Even a trick that small, she'd always had to ask.

Hal took his spectacles off.

"That," he said, "is not a cheap thing you're doing."

"I know." The salt cellar did a little loop. Somewhere in the scroll a hundred voices said *ooooh* in the same breath. "I know, isn't it lovely?"

The mill at the bottom of Crake had lost its wheel in the six days of rain. Everybody in the market was talking about it: the Sallow had come up over the race, the old oak axle had split, and the great undershot wheel had come off and lay half in the millrace like a dead thing, with the water pouring through its spokes. The miller and his sons and about twenty neighbours had ropes on it, and a team of drays, and they were shouting at each other.

Bry stood on the footbridge above the race with her hands in her pockets and the warm coin in her fist.

"Chat," she said, "what do we think?"

The vote closed before she had finished asking. It had never closed before she had finished asking. She was used to the scroll chewing a question over, the regulars arguing, Lantern suggesting left, the Gentlemen dividing five to four. This time the brass came down all at once, like a single word said by a very large mouth, and the power went into her arms so fast she staggered.

"All right," she said. "All right, all right, *lift*."

The wheel came up out of the race.

It came up streaming, with weed hanging off its paddles and the broken axle trailing, three tons of wet oak and iron, and the miller's ropes went slack in twenty pairs of hands. She heard the men on the bank stop shouting. She heard one of the drays scream. She held the thing in the air over the race for a long breath, the water running off it in curtains, and then she set it down on the bank, on its side, gently, like putting a sleeping child into a cot.

The bank was silent. Then somebody started clapping, and somebody else, and then the miller's youngest son, who was perhaps eight, began to cheer, and the scroll went so bright she couldn't read it at all.

Nine hundred and sixty.

She bowed. She always bowed.

---

She spent the rest of the day doing things she had always wanted to try. She played the market square at noon to a crowd that filled it from the well to the Guildhall steps, and when a gust took a fruit-seller's awning she caught it in the air without even looking. She sang four songs, and Chat requested them, and that was strange too, because the requests didn't argue: the brass asked for "The Ferryman's Daughter" and then everyone asked for it, and she sang it.

It was only when she was walking back up the hill to the Brindle that she noticed Grandmother Cinder hadn't said anything.

Cinder didn't say much. That was the point of Cinder. Most days she said one thing, in lowercase, without any punctuation, and it was usually correct, and her sparks came in a long grey fall like ash out of a chimney, enough to light the whole of the Gull for a week. But Cinder always said her one thing.

Bry scrolled back with her eyes, which made her walk into a hitching post. There was so much. Brass, brass, *good morning friend*, *LIFT*, a few lines from the Gentlemen, a long complaint from Thimble about a lewd new imp it had hushed. No Cinder. The count of grey sparks for the day was nothing at all.

"Cinder?" she said. "Grandmother? You there?"

The scroll went on. *we love you bry*, said a hundred friends at once, which was nice of them.

Mothlight didn't answer, and the brass came over the place where it had been.

In the evening, at the moment the lamplighter of Crake started on the bottom of the high street with his pole and his little flame, Bry stood in the inn yard and lit every lamp in the town.

She hadn't planned it. The vote just came: *LIGHTS*, the brass said, all together, and she lifted her hand, and up and down the hill, in the square, on the bridges, on the corners, three hundred lamps caught at once with a soft sound like a crowd drawing breath. The lamplighter stood at the bottom of the street with his pole in his hand and his mouth open.

When she turned round Corvin was leaning in the inn's back door with his arms folded, the cord of thorn beads dark at his throat.

"Well?" Bry said.

He said nothing.

"You could say it was good. It *was* good. The man's wheel is out of the water. The boy cheered."

"It was good," Corvin said.

"Say it like you mean it, then."

"I said what I had to say in the market yesterday." He didn't move from the doorway. "You didn't want to hear it then. I don't suppose you want to hear it twice."

She did a little turn in the lamplight with her arms out. Nine hundred and ninety. "You know what I think?" she said. "I think you've been on your own with your silent lady so long you can't stand to see someone liked."

Something moved in his face and was put away.

"Perhaps," he said, and went inside.

She wrote in the guestbook in bed, the way she always did, with the candle guttering and the book propped on her knees, its spine creaking. She liked this part: each new voice that had spoken got its line, a name and a note. *Lantern-in-the-Fen: always says go left. The Nine Wet Gentlemen: do not mention the cook.* Seven years of names.

She asked again, to be thorough. "Right, new people. One at a time. Names, please, for the book."

"You can't all be called that."

She laughed and wrote, in her big untidy hand, at the bottom of the page: *Just a Friend. (Lots.) Likes: everything. Hates: nothing.*

She looked at it for a while. It was the shortest line on the page and it had cost her the most ink to make look like a joke.

Then she blew out the candle, and lay down, and the scroll ran on in the dark the way it always had, the way it had in the garret and in the alley and in every bed since. She was so tired her teeth didn't ache any more. She heard Thimble mutter something and go quiet in the tinderbox on the sill.

*goodnight, bry*, said the friends.

Every one of them, all at once, in exactly the same breath.
