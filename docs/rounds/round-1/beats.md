1. punch-or-fire: Punch or Fire
   Cold open, waist-deep in Marrow Fen. Jess fights a bog-wight for nine crowns, narrating every move aloud to Chat: Gristlemouth demanding she bite it, Mothlight rating her form, the Widow fussing, Brindle greeting her first as always, Twelve-Pence counting the bounty. She calls a poll, 'punch or fire', chat votes fire, the power floods in, the wight burns, and she slips and falls in the peat anyway. The rules of her magic shown, not told: tithes, polls, backlash. Last image: from the reeds, a woman in a Vigilant's grey watches the warlock thank an empty fen.
2. get-this-straight: Let Me Get This Straight
   The fen-village inn. Odile arrests Jess over a bowl of eel stew, by the book, warrant read in full. In the lockup Jess, who has never been arrested by anyone so polite, explains: no great patron, hundreds of small ones, all watching right now. Odile says the line. 'Chat,' says Jess, and explains the playhouse gallery. Chat's reaction to Odile scrolls past (they adore her; Mothlight proposes a poll on her eyebrows). Jess tells the version she always tells: she was an actress, she dried on a stage in Saltreach, she made up a line. Odile writes it down as evidence.
3. wheres-brindle: Where's Brindle?
   The road to Aurenne, chained wrist to wrist. Jess can't stop talking, because she can't: she narrates breakfast, the weather, Odile's horse. Odile prays at night to a god that has never answered and to which, she says, silence is an answer. Jess realises Brindle hasn't said good morning in two days. Twelve-Pence, evasive, admits several regulars have gone quiet this spring. Jess makes a bit out of it, a mock funeral for a dog-spirit, and chat laughs, and she lies awake.
4. coldharbour: Coldharbour Ferry
   At the Sedge ferry Lucan Thrale steps off the far bank with two of the Ledger's hired Lessers. Jess knows him at once and goes white. Chained together, Jess and Odile fight as a pair, badly then well; Jess calls a poll mid-fight ('left or water') and chat's vote throws them both into the river and out of reach. Lucan, on the bank: the Ledger is buying her, piece by piece, and there is nothing she can do. 'You said a line that wasn't in the script, Jess. Somebody had to pay for it.' He is gone before she can ask what he means. Downstream the ferryman, Abel Drury, hauls them out with a boat-hook and dries them by his stove without a single question, and looks at Jess a moment too long, as if he has dreamt her.
5. three-questions: Three Questions
   In a ruined toll-house Jess spends nearly all her saved regard to summon Notary Fifteen with ink and salt. The Register unrolls from its blank face. The Ledger holds 180 of a thousand parts. There are 340 'dormant' parts Jess has never heard of, and 248, the largest single part, held by 'a Witness, through the eyes of another', which has never once spoken in chat. Jess has three questions and wastes one on whether the Notary has a sense of humour (answer: 'Yes.'). When the Notary asks the date and place of the pact for the record, Jess says the Gilded Lamp, Saltreach, third of Frostmonth 612, and Odile goes very still.
6. single-regard: Single Regard
   Odile's chapter. That night she sits up with the prisoner asleep (narrating in her sleep) and remembers: fifteen, over the convent wall, two nights on foot, a smuggled playbill for 'The Lantern Bride', the pit of the Gilded Lamp, a masked girl on the stage saying a line that made the whole house hold its breath, then fire. She was whipped for it and told nobody where she had been. She prays and is not answered. In the morning she unchains Jess, reasoning precisely that the Ledger's claim on a prisoner of the Synod is an offence against the Synod, and makes her swear to walk to Aurenne of her own will. She does not tell her about the pit.
7. gallery-one: The Gallery (I)
   Interlude in the Beyond. The patrons in their copy of the Gilded Lamp's upper gallery, watching Jess sleep on a stage that is her eyelids. Gristlemouth shredding a broker's card ('I DON'T SELL TO LANDLORDS'); Mothlight pocketing his, airily; the Widow knitting something that isn't there; Old Sedge asleep across three benches. Twelve-Pence confesses to the Widow that he has known of the buying since winter and hid it from Jess so she would not be frightened. At the far end of the bench there is a place nobody sits in, where it always feels as though someone is watching the watchers.
8. smoke-at-pennyfold: Smoke at Pennyfold
   Jess detours to the dead mining village and spends a whole day rebuilding Twelve-Pence's hearth-shrine out of fallen stone, while chat argues masonry, tithes for every course, and Odile, who has never seen a small god honoured, carries stones in silence. Jess puts the worn tin penny she wears on a string into the shrine; Twelve-Pence, overcome, gives it back to her through the warmth of the stones: keep it, keep me. Smoke goes up from a Pennyfold hearth for the first time in twenty-one years.
9. irregular: Irregular
   Morning in Pennyfold. Brother Cray rides in, mild and courteous, with four Vigilants and an order in the Archvigilant's own hand that the warlock be killed where found. Odile reads it twice. It is irregular: her warrant says alive, for trial. She refuses to give Jess up until the order is confirmed in Aurenne. Cray smiles, says of course, and lets them ride on. Jess, who reads crowds for a living, tells Odile he will not wait.
10. hask-bridge: Hask Bridge
   Cray's men ambush them on the Widow's own bridge. Jess calls a poll; the power comes; then mid-spell Mothlight sells his part ('lol sorry bestie, it's a pension') and three dozen Lessers bolt after him in a panic. The poll's power collapses under her and she goes over the parapet into the Sedge. The Widow, on the bridge she has kept for two hundred years, catches her, and spends most of herself doing it. Odile holds the bridge alone with a sword and kills a Vigilant, her first death. End of the first act.
11. what-the-widow-spent: What the Widow Spent
   A hayloft above a drovers' inn. The Widow's voice in chat is faint now. Jess tries jokes and they land on nobody. Odile washes another Vigilant's blood out of her surcoat and her hands shake. Their first real conversation: 'Why do you never stop talking?' 'Why do you never start?' Jess, for a moment, nearly tells the truth about something, and then does a bit instead.
12. asked-exactly: Asked Exactly
   Jess has nothing left to pay a Notary; Odile pays with her Vigilant's stipend, which she is not allowed to do. Asked exactly, the Register answers: the dormant parts belong to the mortals who witnessed the pact, the whole house of the Gilded Lamp; whoever holds more than half may call the pact; the Ledger now holds 241. Unasked, Notary Fifteen remarks that the open register has lately received several mortal parts. Jess needs the list of who was in that house. Odile, realising that she herself holds one of those parts, says nothing.
13. the-lamp: The Lamp
   Saltreach. Jess goes back to the burned shell of the Gilded Lamp for the first time in seven years, with Chat, who live in a copy of its gallery, seeing the original at last. Through her memory we have the pact night as she believes it: the Bride's mask, the full house, a devil's presence in the wings, the binding line gone out of her head, dead air, and then a line that came from nowhere. In the ash of the box office she finds a fresh bill for 'Master Bellweather's Marionettes, Lockwater Quay', lettered in Quell's unmistakable hand.
14. no-mercy: No Mercy
   Short of regard after another wave of sales, Jess takes a bout in the Ropewalk fighting pit because Gristlemouth swears it will hold chat. The poll says 'no mercy'. She wins, and breaks the knee of a fighter barely seventeen because the vote said to. Chat's regard spikes; nobody sells that night. Odile watches the whole bout and afterwards says nothing at all, which is worse than anything she could have said.
15. bellweather: Master Bellweather
   Lockwater quay. Jess finds Quell working marionettes for children: a lantern bride, a devil, a prompter's box. He tries charm, then grief, then confesses: he pledged 'the best of my company' and he meant her, and he never told her. He gives her the house book. Asked about the promptbook, he says it burned. He is lying, and Odile, who has watched liars under oath, sees it.
16. dead-names: The Dead Names
   A Lockwater lodging-house, late. Odile, who reads documents for pleasure, sets the house book against parish notices and sees it: eleven of the audience have died in the last eight months, all without heirs, all by 'accident' or fever. Someone is killing Jess's dormant holders so their parts fall onto the open register for the Ledger to claim. Jess, who has never been responsible for anyone but herself and two hundred gods, understands that three hundred strangers are dying of her.
17. hello-is-this-right: Hello? Is This Right?
   The next living name is Mags Tolley, a baker in Lockwater itself. Jess and Odile reach her an hour ahead of Cray. Jess tells her what she holds; Mags, who has dreamt of a girl talking to the dark for seven years, wills it, and her part wakes, and for the first time a mortal speaks in chat: 'hello? is this right? hello'. Chat goes berserk with welcome. Cray comes through the bakehouse door; they fight him off among the ovens; he escapes with his list.
18. columns: Columns
   Lucan's chapter. The Ledger's counting-house in the Beyond, endless desks and one window. Lucan reports. In the Ledger's open book, on the facing page, he sees the Archvigilant's name and a list of mortals with lines drawn through them. She orders him to see to the holders Cray has not reached. He refuses to kill. She does not raise her voice: she takes a year of his life and enters it in a column. We learn he has hated Jess for seven years; we do not learn why.
19. the-witness: The Witness
   Quell's two parts, Mags's waking and a pooled tithe from chat buy one direct question: who is the Witness? The Register names the Unblinking, through the eyes of Odile Varenne, novice, in the pit of the Gilded Lamp. Odile, who has prayed every night of her life to a god that never answered, learns it has been watching Jess for seven years. Jess learns Odile was there that night, and kept it from her.
20. everything-a-show: Everything's a Show to You
   The quarrel, on the towpath at night. Odile: Jess makes a show of everything, even this, even her. Jess: Odile loves a god that would rather watch a stranger than speak to her. Both are right enough to be unforgivable. Odile rides for Aurenne to ask the Archvigilant what it means. Chat, for once, is silent.
21. terms: Terms
   The Ledger comes in person, to a tollhouse on the Sedge road, as courteous as a banker. She tells Jess the story of the tally-god of Scorry who learned thrift. She offers terms: sell the rest willingly and she will release Lucan and pension every Lesser who still watches. She offers Gristlemouth, separately, in front of everyone, the deed to the Ninth Pit of Orrow; he tells her, in chat and loudly, what she can do with it. Jess nearly says yes for Lucan's sake, which surprises her. Chat begs her not to. She asks for time. 'Take a week,' says the Ledger. 'I'll be counting.'
22. gallery-two: The Gallery (II)
   Interlude. The Gallery half empty. Chat splits: Gristlemouth wants bigger fights to hold the numbers, the Widow and Twelve-Pence want Jess to stop and sleep. Old Sedge wakes, for the first time in the book, and says something enormous: he was nearly gathered, six hundred years ago, with all the little gods of the Marches; he was too slow to be caught. 'They took the quick ones. They put them all in one mouth. And the mouth has never said a word.' Nobody understands him except, perhaps, the empty seat at the end of the bench.
23. library-under-the-eye: The Library Under the Eye
   Aurenne. Odile confronts Hesk among the Synod's inner records. He tells her the truth, almost kindly: the Gathering bound a thousand gods into one, the god cannot agree on a word, and that silence is the peace she was born into. He knew she was in the pit; he sent her to Jess so she would be at hand to sign the god's part to the Ledger, for the peace. He calls it a test of her vow. She refuses. She is taken down to the cells under the Eye, still wearing her medal.
24. treadmill: The Treadmill
   Without Odile and losing patrons daily, Jess performs without stopping: three bounties in four days, a fire-walk in a market square, a whole night narrating to keep regard up. She stops eating. Chat fractures into those who want more and those begging her to stop; Gristlemouth, for once, has nothing to shout. Told as a single breathless run of days.
25. crossing: Crossing
   A fairground, a platform of her own making, a crowd of mortals who can't see the gods watching. Jess collapses mid-act. The Widow spends the last of herself to keep Jess's heart going. Her last words in chat are the Hask Bridge saying: 'Mind the step, love; the other side's the same as this one, only dry.' She is gone, and her eight parts with her.
26. seven-years: Seven Years
   Lucan finds Jess alone in a fairground wagon, too weak to fight, and doesn't take her. They talk for the first time in seven years: the Gallimaufry, Quell, the night of the fire, what the Ledger is. He tells her it was never about one soul: she is a precedent, proof that the Lessers don't need the Peerage, and the Ledger must own her and end her in public. The Ledger holds 452. Odile is in a cell under the Eye. Asked why he hates her, he leaves.
27. honest-stream: Nobody's Watching (Everybody's Watching)
   Dawn on the wagon steps. For the first time since the pact Jess does not perform. She tells Chat everything: the numbers, the Ledger's offer, that she is afraid, that she was a pickpocket Quell turned into a show, that she doesn't know who she is when nobody is watching and has made sure never to find out. It is the quietest stream of her life. Nobody sells. Two Lessers who sold write from the Ledger's pension asking if they can come back. Mags says she's proud of her. Gristlemouth says, very quietly for him, 'still here'. At noon Jess writes the Ledger her answer to the terms, one word, and doesn't read it out.
28. the-house-book: The House Book
   Jess, Mags and, to everyone's surprise, Quell, who has followed them up the Sedge with the promptbook and a guilty face, work through the house book name by name, waking dormant holders. At Coldharbour the ferryman Drury, whose name is in the book beside his late wife's, says 'I knew I'd dreamt you' and wakes his part. Two seamstresses; Judge Halloway, eighty, who sat thirty years on the Cathedral's fabric committee and talks about its drains with love; Pip, who was six that night and becomes chat's loudest member within an hour. On the way, unannounced and off the scroll, Jess goes back to the Ropewalk and pays for Tam Rook's knee. Quell will not yet open the promptbook.
29. gallery-three: The Gallery (III)
   Interlude. The Gallery fuller than it has ever been: woken mortals sitting on the benches beside small gods, Mags next to Twelve-Pence, the ferryman arguing tides with Old Sedge. Twelve-Pence teaches Mags what a hush is. The Widow's place is kept. At the end of the bench the empty seat is, for the first time, slightly leaned toward the stage.
30. into-the-eye: Into the Eye
   The heist. With the magistrate's memory of the Cathedral's drains and chat, mortal and Lesser, polling every corridor ('left or stairs', 'wait or run'), Jess goes under the Eye after Odile. Done fast and funny, chat at its best; Odile found in a cell carved with eyes, still praying.
31. one-exact-act: One Exact Act
   The cell door is warded beyond anything Jess can break, and her regard is spent. Twelve-Pence sells his nine parts to the Ledger, all at once, takes their price in regard, and spends it on one small exact act: the lock opens. He is gone from the Gallery. Jess still has his penny, warm. Above them, in the chapter-house, Hesk hands the Ledger the last of Cray's murdered parts; with Twelve-Pence's nine she passes half, and calls the pact. Halfway up the drain stair, with Odile's hand in hers, Jess stops walking.
32. lantern-bride-again: The Lantern Bride, Again
   The nave of the Cathedral, packed with the faithful for the public trial of a warlock. The Ledger, holding more than half, means to rewrite the pact so every part is hers, and a pact is rewritten in the warlock's own mouth. She has Jess put on the scorched Bride's mask and perform the pact scene from 'The Lantern Bride' as first written. Jess's body says the lines. Chat screams in her head. Quell, in the crowd, sees what his surety has made of her. Odile, in chains beside Hesk, watches the god's window close on everything.
33. prompt: Prompt
   As the Bride reaches the binding line, Lucan steps out of the Ledger's train and tells Jess the truth before three thousand witnesses: seven years ago he fed her that line from the prompt corner, he paid for it, he has hated her for it every day since, and he is sorry. Then he does it again: he prompts her aloud, the wrong line, a nonsense line from the second act, and the called body stumbles, and the rewrite stalls for the moment Odile needs.
34. ink-salt-blood: Ink, Salt, Blood
   Odile, with ink from Hesk's own desk, salt from the altar and her own blood for the fee, summons Notary Fifteen into the nave and lays a claim under the Concordance. The evidence: the house book; Cray's list in Hesk's hand; Lucan's testimony that the Ledger ordered the killings; and Quell, opening the promptbook at last, the real contract pinned in its back board and his confession that Jess never knew what he pledged. The Notary rules: every part claimed off a murdered holder is void; Quell's surety was void from the start; so Lucan's damages were never owed. The Ledger drops below half. The call breaks.
35. a-thousand-words: A Thousand Words
   Jess, free and shaking, puts one question to every holder: rewrite the pact as a commons, never to be sold or called. The Lessers and the woken mortals vote. It is not enough without the Witness. Odile takes off her medal, the formal breaking of her vow, and looks at Jess freely, and her own part wakes, and with it the god's: a thousand voices that have not spoken in six hundred years pour into chat at once, each saying its own word, and every one of them votes yes. Jess reads some of them aloud for Odile. Every Vigilant in the nave hears it too.
36. default: Default
   The rewrite returns every sold part to its first holder. The Ledger is left owing pensions on parts she no longer holds: a debt that can never be paid, the one thing a debt-god cannot survive. Notary Fifteen opens a new page with her name at the top. Brindle says good morning. Mothlight comes back sheepish. Twelve-Pence comes back last and says, exactly, that he has been gone for nine hours and fourteen minutes.
37. going-dark: Going Dark
   Midsummer. Hesk stripped of office by Vigilants who heard their god speak; Cray in the Crown's custody; the Synod in uproar about a god that will not stop talking. Jess pays Twelve-Pence his penny at the Pennyfold shrine. By vote of the commons she may go dark one night a week. On the first, she sleeps eight hours beside Odile and wakes to find Chat has kept talking among themselves all night, and that a new name in the scroll, Lucan's, has written: 'prompt: you're doing fine.'
