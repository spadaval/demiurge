The Sedge was in spring flood at Coldharbour, brown and fast and full of other people's fences, and the ferry was on the wrong side of it.

"There's a bell," said Odile. She pointed with her free hand, the left; her right was chained to Jess's left, and had been for four days, and the two of them had learned to point with the other one. "On the post. One rings for the ferryman."

"One does," Jess agreed, and rang it. Then, to the air: "Coldharbour Ferry, everyone. Famous for nothing. There's a ferryman somewhere called Drury, according to the man at the inn, who said, and I'm quoting, 'he's big and he doesn't talk.' So Ser Odile and Drury are going to get on beautifully."

*lol,* said someone. Not many someones. Behind her eyes the scroll went up thin this morning, a line here and a line there, with long dark gaps between, like the last of a fire. She had stopped counting the gaps on the second day. She had stopped looking for Brindle's name on the third.

*She's right though,* said Mothlight. *Two of them, not talking at each other. I'd watch that.*

"You'd watch anything."

"I didn't speak," said Odile.

"I know. I wasn't talking to you."

"I know," said Odile, who did, by now, and had stopped writing it down every time. She stood with her feet apart on the shingle and watched the far bank with the patient, unexcited attention of a woman watching a door she has been told someone will come through. She did not look at Jess. She had a way of not looking at Jess that Jess had begun, against her will, to find restful. Everybody else in the world was always looking.

On the far bank the ferry moved.

It was a flat raft on a rope, the kind you hauled across hand over hand, and someone was hauling it. Not a big man who didn't talk. A slim one, in a long dark coat, working the rope easily, as though he'd been doing it all his life, with two passengers standing behind him very still. The current shoved at the raft. It swung and steadied. The slim man laughed at something and pulled it straight.

Jess knew the laugh before she knew the face.

You don't forget the people you've been on a stage with. You learn their breathing. You learn the half-second before they speak, and whether they'll come in early or late, and which side they'll turn to when they've forgotten the next line. She had stood two feet from that laugh every night for six seasons and she knew it as well as her own voice.

The raft grounded on their bank with a crunch, and Lucan Thrale stepped off it onto the shingle and smiled at her.

"Jess," he said.

He'd got thinner. The good coat was very good. His hands were stained at the fingertips, all ten of them, dark grey, as if he'd been writing for a long time with a pen that leaked.

"Lucan," said Jess. Her face had gone cold. She could feel it doing it, the blood going out of her cheeks, and she could not stop it, and there was nothing behind her eyes but a sudden wall of names, sixty, eighty, a hundred of them waking up at once.

*who's that*

*Jess, who is that*

*oh no,* said the Widow, who knew. *Oh, love.*

"You know this man," said Odile.

"We were in a company together."

"You were in a company together," Lucan agreed pleasantly. "We were very good. She was better." He looked at the chain and at Odile's grey and at the open silver eye over her heart, and his eyebrows went up. "And now she's in the Synod's company. That's a step down for you, Jess."

"Sir," said Odile. "This woman is a prisoner of the Synod under warrant. Stand aside from the landing."

"I'd love to," said Lucan. "I'm afraid I'm here on business." He tipped his head a little to one side, as if he were listening to somebody count. "My patron would like a word with Miss Pell. Several words. She's terribly interested in Miss Pell."

"Your patron," said Jess.

"You remember. You must have heard. The Ledger." He said it the way a clerk says the name of the house he works for, without love. "It's a living."

The two passengers had stepped off the raft behind him. They were dressed as millers, in floury aprons, and they were the wrong shape for their clothes. Their faces were white and round and grooved in rings, and when they moved they moved with a long grinding slowness, as though the ground had to agree to it first.

Behind Jess's eyes, someone said: *Cobble?*

And then a dozen people said it.

*COBBLE*

*that's Cobble, that's Old Cobble from Fenner's Mill*

*cobble what are you DOING*

*He used to watch,* said Twelve-Pence, very quietly. *He watched every day. He went quiet at Midwinter.*

The bigger of the two millstone-men turned its grooved face toward Jess, as if it could hear them. Perhaps it could. It didn't say anything. It looked, if a millstone could look anything, ashamed.

"He's not in the gallery anymore," said Lucan. "He's in the Ledger's. Pensioned. Fed every day, looked after, never has to listen to you again." He spread his stained hands. "That's what she's doing, Jess. She's buying you. Piece by piece, voice by voice. How many did you have this morning? Fewer than at Thawmonth? Fewer than last week? You've noticed. You're not stupid. You're just too busy talking to listen."

"Seize her," he said to the millers, almost gently, and they came.

The first thing Jess learned about fighting chained to a Vigilant was that the Vigilant would go one way and she would go the other. Odile's sword came out of its scabbard right-handed, which was the chained hand, which yanked Jess's arm across her body and spun her half round; Jess lunged left, out of instinct, which dragged Odile's sword-arm down; and the first millstone-man's fist, which was the size and weight of a quernstone, came down into the shingle between them and made the whole beach jump.

"Left hand!" Jess yelled. "Use your left!"

"I am *trained* right-handed—"

"Then I'll go right—"

They both went right. The second millstone-man was there. Jess got her free hand up full of whatever chat had in it, which this morning was not much, a thin hot spark from Gristlemouth and a tremor of luck from Mothlight, and slapped it into the grooved face, and the millstone-man rocked back one slow inch and came on.

*FIGHT,* said Gristlemouth, who had been waiting for this all week. *FIGHT IT. HIT THE TRAITOR.*

*Don't hurt Cobble,* said someone else. *Please don't hurt him.*

"I'm trying to hurt *anyone*—"

Odile stopped arguing. Jess felt it through the chain: the moment the knight decided. Odile switched her sword to her left hand without a word and stepped *in*, close, her chained hand flat against Jess's back, so they stood shoulder to shoulder, facing out two ways, like dancers at the turn of a figure. "When I pull," Odile said, low, "duck."

She pulled. Jess ducked. Odile's sword went over her head and opened a long white groove across the second millstone's face, and grit sprayed, and the thing went down on one knee with a sound like a cart going over a bridge.

They were good together for perhaps four seconds. It was the best four seconds Jess had had in weeks.

Then the first one, Cobble, caught the chain.

He didn't pull. He just held it, and pressed down, slowly, and the weight of a millstone came onto both their wrists, and both of them went to their knees on the shingle.

Lucan had not moved from the landing. He watched them with his head on one side, listening to his sums.

Jess closed her eyes.

"Poll," she said, and her voice came out cracked, which she would hate herself for later. "Quick one. Left — or water."

It was not a question that made sense. They answered it anyway. They always did. Two columns came up behind her eyes and ran; *LEFT*, said Gristlemouth, *LEFT AND HIT HIM*, but the other column ran faster and fuller, Twelve-Pence and the Widow and Mothlight and the stoat-god and every quiet name that hadn't said a word all week, *water, water, water*, all of them pouring their little portions into the same word.

It came into her like a tide coming over a weir.

"Hold your breath," she told Odile, and stood up under the weight of a millstone as if it were a coat, and took two long steps backwards off the edge of the shingle into the Sedge in flood, and the river took them both.

It was cold enough to stop her heart. The brown water closed over her head and turned her over and over, and the chain jerked tight, and Odile was a dark shape tumbling at the end of it, and the power chat had given her burned in her chest like a swallowed lamp and *kept them up*, kept their faces toward the light, kept the river from putting them under the fences and the drowned branches, carried them out and down and away, faster than any man could run along a bank.

She got her head up once. The landing was already small. Lucan stood at the edge of the shingle with his coat-tails flapping, not running, not shouting for the millers, only watching her go, with an expression she had never seen on his face in six seasons on a stage, and could not name.

He cupped his hands and called after her across the water, quite clearly:

"You said a line that wasn't in the script, Jess! Somebody had to pay for it!"

"What?" she shouted, and swallowed half the Sedge. "*What line?* Lucan—"

The river turned her round. When it turned her back, the landing was empty. The raft was drifting loose on its rope. There was no one on the bank at all.

The ferryman found them at the bend below his cottage, hung up in a willow, half drowned. He was as big as advertised. He leaned out of his punt with a long iron boat-hook, caught the chain between them in its crook, and hauled them both aboard like a catch of something he hadn't meant to land, and asked nothing.

He sat them by his stove in two blankets each. He put a mug of something hot and brown into Odile's left hand and one into Jess's right, so that neither of them had to let go of the other to drink. He did not ask why a knight of the Synod was chained to a woman with river-weed in her hair who kept saying "thank you, thank you, I know, I know, I'm all right" to no one at all.

He did not say anything, in fact, until he was hanging their cloaks on the drying-rack. Then he stopped, with Jess's cloak in his hands, and looked at her. He looked at her for rather longer than a stranger should. It was not an unkind look. It was the look of a man who has been trying for a long while to remember where he has seen a face, and has just been told the answer, and does not believe it.

"Hm," said Abel Drury, and hung up the cloak, and went out to see to his boat.
