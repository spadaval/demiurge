They did it in Mags Tolley's bakehouse after the last batch was out, because a bakehouse has salt by the sack and a clean floor, and because Mags had said, "Well, you're not doing a summoning in my front parlour," and then gone up to bed and left them the key.

The ovens ticked as they cooled. Flour lay on everything like a light snow. Jess knelt on the swept boards and drew the circle in ink with a baster, because it was what there was, and Odile poured the salt round it in a line so straight it might have been ruled.

"You've done this before," said Jess.

"I watched you do it twice." Odile set down the sack. "I watch carefully."

"I know you do."

There was regard pooled in Jess's chest, more than she'd held in a month. It did not all feel like hers. Some of it was Quell's: two parts, woken that afternoon on Lockwater quay with his hand shaking on the rail of his puppet booth, tithed across to her in one grudging, guilty lump that tasted of greasepaint and cheap brandy. Some was Mags's single part, warm and floury and plain. And the rest was chat, every voice that was left, each of them putting in what they could spare, the way a gallery passes the hat for a player who's fallen on hard times.

*for the question,* Twelve-Pence had said, that morning, counting it in. *One question. Ask it exactly.*

*ask who it is,* said Gristlemouth. *ask who it is and then we'll go and FIGHT it.*

*Nobody is fighting anybody,* said the Widow, faintly. She was always faint now, since the bridge.

"All right," said Jess. She looked up at Odile. "Ready?"

"I am not the one asking."

"You're the one who has to watch, though." She tried a grin. It came out wrong. "I'm told you're good at it."

She cut her thumb on the bread-knife and let one drop fall into the ink, and spoke the Notary's name, and opened her hand, and the regard went out of her all at once like breath.

The bakehouse got colder. The flour on the boards lifted in a slow ring and settled again. And Notary Fifteen stood in the circle, as it had stood in the ruined toll-house and in the hayloft above the drovers' inn: tall, in clerk's black, with no face at all, only a smooth oval of blank paper where a face should be, faintly ruled.

"Miss Pell," it said. It did not have a mouth. The voice came out of the paper anyway, dry and clear, like someone reading aloud in the next room. "Ser Varenne. You have paid for one question. Ask."

Jess had rehearsed it all afternoon. She had said it to the river, to the quay, to the empty barges, to chat, who had argued every word of it like a committee.

"The part of my pact held by the one you list as 'a Witness, through the eyes of another'," she said. "Who is the Witness, and through whose eyes?"

"That is two questions."

"It's one question with a comma in it."

There was a pause. The blank face tilted, very slightly.

"So it is," said Notary Fifteen.

It lifted its hands. From the bottom edge of its face the paper began to come, as it had before, unrolling slowly down over its chest and its folded hands and onto the floor, a long pale tongue of it, close-written in a small brown hand: the Register, the whole of her, a thousand parts in columns. Jess had seen it twice and it still turned her stomach. Names went by. The Ledger's name went by, too many times. Brindle's, struck through. Mothlight's, struck through. Cobble's.

The paper stopped. One entry lay uppermost on the boards in the lamplight, longer than the rest.

The Notary read it aloud, without inflection, the way it read everything.

"*Two hundred and forty-eight parts. Held by the Witness called the Unblinking, the god of the Synod of the Eye, being one god of a thousand; who witnessed the binding through the eyes of Odile Varenne, novice of the Vigilant convent at Aurenne, standing in the pit of the Gilded Lamp, by her vow its window. Which parts may be woken, voted or conveyed only through the said Odile Varenne, and not otherwise. Item: one further part, dormant, held by the said Odile Varenne in her own right.*"

The ovens ticked.

Behind Jess's eyes, for one long moment, there was nothing at all. Not a name. Not a line. The whole gallery holding its breath.

Then it broke.

*THE GOD???*

*the church god*

*the CHURCH GOD is in CHAT*

*it's the seat,* said Twelve-Pence, quite quietly, under all of it. *Jess. It's the seat at the end of the bench. The one nobody sits in.*

*She was THERE?* said Gristlemouth. *The knight was THERE?*

*Oh, love,* said the Widow, and Jess didn't know which of them she meant.

Jess wasn't looking at the Register anymore. She was looking at Odile.

Odile had not moved. She stood at the edge of the salt with her hands at her sides and the silver eye over her heart catching the lamplight, and she was looking at the paper on the floor as if it were a letter in her own handwriting that she had no memory of writing. Her face had done nothing at all. Jess had spent her life reading faces from forty feet away under bad lights. She had never seen one do so much nothing.

"You were in the pit," Jess said.

"Yes."

"At the Lamp. On the night."

"Yes."

"How long have you known?"

Odile's eyes came up off the paper. "That I was there? Since I was fifteen. That it was you on the stage?" A very small breath. "Since the toll-house. When the Notary asked you the date and the place for its record, and you said the third of Frostmonth, the Gilded Lamp. I knew then."

"That was a *month* ago."

"Twenty-four days."

"Odile."

"I climbed the convent wall," Odile said, as if Jess had asked her something else entirely. Her voice was very level. "A girl in the scullery had a playbill. *The Lantern Bride*, by the Gallimaufry Players, with Miss Pellegrine. I had never seen a play. I walked two nights to Saltreach and paid a penny to stand in the pit, and a girl in a mask stood up on the stage and said a line, and the whole house went still, and I have never felt anything like it before or since. Then the lamps went up. I walked back. They whipped me for it. I didn't tell them where I'd been." She looked at the paper. "I have never told anyone where I'd been."

"You could have told *me*."

"At the second reading the Notary said that the house of the Gilded Lamp held parts in your pact." Odile spoke as if giving evidence. "A Vigilant who holds a part in a warlock's pact is an apostate. She is stripped of her eye and turned out. I did not know what to do. So I did nothing. I am" — the smallest hitch — "I am sorry."

"*It's* not sorry," Jess said, and jerked her chin at the paper, and only then understood what she had said.

Odile understood it at the same moment. Jess watched it reach her.

"We are its windows," Odile said. "That is the vow. Single regard. We look at nothing else, so that it can look through us. That is all the vow is." She was speaking very slowly now. "I was a novice. I had taken the lesser vow. It was looking through me. In the pit." A pause. "At you."

"Odile—"

"And it has been watching you since." Not a question. "Every day. For seven years."

Jess opened her mouth and could not, for once in her life, find a single thing to say into it.

*Tell her,* said Twelve-Pence. *She should know. Tell her it's true.*

"It's never said anything," Jess said instead. "Not once. It's—it doesn't talk. We didn't even know it was there, not really. There's a place at the end of the bench nobody sits in, and it feels like being looked at, that's all. That's all it is. It's never said a word."

"No," said Odile. "It doesn't."

She went down on one knee beside the Register. Jess thought she was going to touch it, and Notary Fifteen thought so too, because its blank face turned toward her, but she didn't. She only knelt there and read the entry again, her lips moving, the way Jess had seen her read her own warrant in the fen-village lockup, as though if she read it carefully enough it would turn out to say something else.

"Every night," Odile said, to the paper, "since I was eleven years old. Every night of my life I have knelt down and said the office and waited, and it has never once answered. Not once. And I told myself that was the answer. That a god who sees everything doesn't need to speak to a girl in a cell. That its silence was a kind of—attention." She stopped. "It was attending. Just not to me."

Behind Jess's eyes chat had gone quiet again, but it was a different quiet: the hush a house falls into when a player stops acting and the audience isn't sure, yet, whether it's allowed to look.

And Jess, who had been on stages since she was six, who had never once in her life been able to bear a silence in front of a house, heard herself say:

"Well. At least you two have that in common. Neither of you ever says anything."

She knew as it left her mouth. You always know, the instant after; you can feel a line die. She felt this one go out across the floury boards and land on Odile's bent back like a slap.

*jess,* said someone. *jess no.*

Odile stood up. She did it carefully, the way she did everything, brushing the flour from the knee of her grey.

"The question is answered," she said to the Notary. "Is there a fee owing?"

"None," said Notary Fifteen. The Register was rolling itself back up, slow and tidy, into the blank of its face. When it was done, the faceless head turned, unmistakably, toward Jess, and stayed there a moment longer than it needed to. "Though I would observe, Miss Pell, as one who was once asked whether it had a sense of humour, that not everything said in a full house is improved by being funny."

Then it was gone, and the circle was only ink and salt on a bakehouse floor.

Odile picked up her sword-belt from the flour-bin and buckled it on.

"I'm going to walk," she said. "By the canal."

"Odile, I didn't—"

"I know you didn't." She paused at the door, her hand on the latch, her back to the room. "That is rather the trouble with you, I think. You never do."

The door closed behind her. The ovens ticked. Jess sat back on her heels in a ring of salt with her cut thumb in her mouth and a hundred and some voices behind her eyes, and for the first time she could remember, not one of them was telling her what to do next.
