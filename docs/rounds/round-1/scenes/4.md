The door was oak, a hand's breadth thick, banded in iron, and there were eyes carved all over it.

Not painted. Carved, deep, each the size of a thumbprint, hundreds of them, open, lidless, cut into the wood and the iron alike in rows so close they touched, and every one of them was looking at Jess. She could feel them do it. It was the feeling of the end of the bench in the Gallery, the seat nobody sat in, multiplied until it pressed on her skin like weather.

Through the grille at head height, Odile looked at her too.

She was on her knees on the stone in her shirtsleeves, the grey surcoat gone, the silver eye still on its chain over her heart. There were eyes carved on the walls of the cell behind her as well, and on the floor, and on the low ceiling, so that she knelt in the middle of being looked at from every side. She had been praying. Jess had heard her, coming down the last of the drain stair: the office, in the old tongue, very steady.

"You came," said Odile.

"I had a poll," said Jess. "It was unanimous. Well. Gristle voted 'burn the cathedral down', but he was outvoted."

*I STAND BY IT,* said Gristlemouth.

Odile's mouth did something it had never done in Jess's sight before. It might, in better light, have been the beginning of a laugh.

Then it stopped, because Jess said, "I'm sorry. For the bakehouse. For the joke. For all of it. I'm sorry," and found that she couldn't make it into anything else, couldn't land it or button it or turn it to the house, could only stand on the wet stone of a drain under the Cathedral of the Eye and say it twice like an idiot.

"I know," said Odile. "I was rude at the door. I have been thinking of a better thing to have said for twenty-six days." She stood up. "Open the door, Jess. We can be sorry on the stairs."

Jess put both hands flat on the oak, between the eyes, and pushed with everything she had.

What she had was very little. The drains had cost her. Every corridor had been a poll and every poll had been a spark, *left or stairs*, *wait or run*, *that guard or the other guard*, and chat had paid for all of them gladly, the woken mortals learning to vote as they went, Pip shrieking her answers half a second before anyone else, the old judge giving directions to culverts he had last seen in a surveyor's drawing forty years ago — *third grating on your right, my dear, and mind the step, it was always a scandal, that step* — and she had spent it all getting here. There was a warmth left in her chest about the size of a coal from last night's fire.

The door did not so much resist it as fail to notice it. The carved eyes drank it like dry sand drinks a cup of water. The lock didn't so much as click.

She tried again. Her nose began to bleed.

"Stop," said Odile, sharp. "Jess. Stop. It's warded with the god. You can't break that. Nobody can."

*She's right,* said Mags, in a small voice. *Love, I think she's right.*

*Poll the house,* said Pip. *Do a poll, do a poll—*

"There's nothing left to poll *with*," Jess said. She put her forehead against the oak. The eyes pressed on her. "Everybody's put in everything. There's nothing."

And behind her eyes, quite quietly, under all the rest of it, Twelve-Pence said:

*There is something.*

She knew. She knew from the way he said it. She had heard that voice before anyone's: the first, out of the hundreds, on the worst night of her life, when the Lamp was burning and she was sitting on the cobbles of Lantern Street in a scorched mask with all the gods of the Sedge shouting in her head at once, and one small grave voice had said, under all of them, *Steady. Count with me. One. Two.* It had counted her down to ten. It had been counting for her ever since.

"No," she said.

*There is a standing offer,* said Twelve-Pence, *for my nine parts. The Ledger's broker made it in Thawmonth. I have it by me. I have always had it by me.*

"No. Pence. *No.*"

*At the standing price,* he said, exactly as he said everything, *nine parts is worth a great deal of regard, all at once. More than I have ever held. Enough for one thing. If it is done exactly.*

*DON'T,* said Gristlemouth, and it was not his fighting voice. *Don't you sell to the landlord. Don't you DARE. Not you.*

*It's a lock,* said Twelve-Pence. *I am very good with locks.*

"She'll have you," Jess said. Her voice had cracked right across and she let it. Behind the grille Odile had gone very still, watching her face. "Pence, if she gets nine more she'll — I don't know how close she is. She could be close. You'll be in her pension, you'll never — you won't be *here*—"

*You put a penny in my hearth,* said Twelve-Pence, *at Pennyfold. Smoke went up. The first in twenty-one years.* A pause. She could almost see him, the way she never could: small, upright, in a miner's coat a century out of fashion. *I have been reckoning, since. I find I have been owing you change.*

"Pence—"

*Hold the penny, Jess. Count with me.*

There was a new line in the scroll. It came up in a hand she had seen three times before, small and brown and even, and it was not addressed to her.

NOTARY FIFTEEN: *Recorded. Nine parts, the hearth-god called Twelve-Pence to the Peer called the Ledger, at the standing price. Paid.*

And then the regard came.

It was not like a tithe. It was not like a poll. It came into her all at once and from one direction, from one small grave voice, more of it than he had ever held in his life, and it did not flood her and it did not burn. It went where he sent it. She felt it go down her arm and into her fingers and from her fingers into the lock, the way a key goes in, the way a coin falls exactly through a slot. It was not force. Nothing was broken. Every carved eye on the oak went on looking.

*One,* said Twelve-Pence.

The lock turned over.

It was the smallest sound she had ever heard. A click like a tongue against teeth.

The door swung inward under her hands and Odile was there, in the doorway, out of the cell, real, her hand already in Jess's, and Jess was looking at the scroll behind her eyes for the next line, for *Two*, for anything.

There was nothing. A gap in the scroll where he had been. A seat on the bench, empty.

She found the penny on its cord at her throat without looking. It was warm. Not warm from her skin; warm the way a hearthstone is warm in the morning, after the fire's gone out.

*He's gone,* said Pip, bewildered. *Where did he — Jess, where did he go?*

Nobody answered her. For a long moment the whole Gallery was silent, mortal and Lesser alike, and the only sound in the drain was the water.

Then Odile's hand tightened on hers.

"Jess," said Odile quietly. "Stairs."

---

In the chapter-house above them, under the tall window, the Archvigilant of the Synod laid a folio on the table between himself and the Ledger and stepped back from it, as a man steps back from a fire he has built.

"Thirty-six," said Corvane Hesk. "Claimed off the open register this month. Brother Cray was thorough. Each one notarised."

The Ledger did not touch the folio. She did not need to. She sat with her ink-dark fingers laced on the table and her grey head a little to one side, as if listening to a column add itself.

"Four hundred and eighty-eight," she said. "Of nine hundred and ninety-two, since the bridge-ghost was so careless as to stop existing. Thank you, Archvigilant. I need eight more than I have, and nine would be tidier."

"The god will forgive what was done for its peace," said Hesk, to no one in particular.

"Your god," said the Ledger, "has no opinion. It has always been its chief charm."

She stopped. Her head tilted further. Somewhere very far off, or very near, a dry voice had said a word, and a line had been written in a book, and she had felt it, as a banker feels a coin land in a drawer three rooms away.

"Ah," said the Ledger softly. "The hearth-god. How very kind of him." She closed her eyes, as if savouring a sum. "Four hundred and ninety-seven."

Hesk said nothing. He had gone rather pale.

The Ledger stood, and smoothed her grey skirts, and spoke, not loudly. She never spoke loudly. She did not need to be heard by Hesk.

---

Halfway up the drain stair, with Odile's hand in hers and water running over her boots and the grating of the Cathedral yard a grey square of night above them, Jess heard it.

Not in the scroll. Not behind her eyes at all. It came from somewhere further in, in the place where she kept her own name; and it was a woman's voice, courteous, exact, like the pages of a ledger turning over one by one in an empty room.

"Miss Pell," it said. "Stand still."

And Jess stood still.

Odile, two steps above, felt the drag on her hand and turned. "Jess. What—"

Jess could not answer. Her mouth was not hers to open. Her feet were not hers to lift. She could only stand on the wet stone, with her eyes open, and her hand in Odile's, and the penny cooling slowly at her throat; and behind her eyes the whole of chat began, all at once, to scream.
