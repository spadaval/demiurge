"Right," said Jess Pell, to nobody, waist-deep in Marrow Fen. "Good morning, everyone. Welcome back. If you're just joining us, I'm standing in a bog."

The bog agreed with her by sucking at her left boot. Mist sat on the water in a low grey lid, and the reeds on every side were taller than she was, so that the whole world was a small brown room with a white ceiling and no walls to speak of. Somewhere in it was the thing she had come for.

Behind her eyes the voices came up, the way they always did, like a house filling before the curtain.

*morning jess!!* That was Brindle, first, as he was always first. *morning morning good morning*

"Morning, Brindle."

*Where's the wight,* said Gristlemouth, who never said good morning to anyone. *Is it big. Tell me it's big.*

*You're up to your hips in water the colour of tea, dear,* said the Widow of Hask Bridge. *Your boots will never forgive you.*

*Nine crowns,* said Twelve-Pence, in his small, grave, counting voice. *The bounty is nine crowns. Less the reeve's tenth, eight crowns and a florin. Less the boots.*

"The boots were already dead, Pence."

*Then eight crowns and a florin.*

She could see them, if she closed her eyes. Not their faces. Their words: pale lines of writing going up the inside of her eyelids like smoke up a chimney, each line wearing the name of whoever had said it, crowding and overlapping and scrolling away into the dark at the top. Two hundred and eighty-seven names. Most mornings perhaps sixty of them were awake enough to talk. The rest just watched, the way the back rows of a house watched, a warmth she could feel on the side of her face like a lamp left burning.

She kept her eyes open. It was a bog.

"So," she said, wading. "Marrow Fen. Peat country. Mostly flat, mostly wet, and mostly owned by the Reeve of Coldmere, who has a problem. The problem has eaten two sheep and a pig and the leg of a man called Hob. Hob is fine. Hob was very clear with me that Hob is fine and that the leg was the bad one anyway. Hob is a hero and I want you all to say something nice about Hob."

*hob!!* said Brindle. *good hob*

*Hob should have run faster,* said Gristlemouth.

*Six out of ten for the opening,* said Mothlight, who rated everything. *You've done the sheep joke before.*

"I have not done the sheep joke before."

*Thawmonth. The barrow-hound. "Two sheep and a vicar."*

"That was a vicar. Completely different shape of joke." She pushed a curtain of reeds aside with the back of her wrist. The water here had a skin on it, rainbow-sheened, and under the skin something had turned over recently and made the silt bloom brown. "Here we go. Here we go. Quiet in the gallery, please."

They were not quiet. They were never quiet. That was the point of them.

*Is it there,* said Gristlemouth. *Is it there is it there is it.*

*Hush, Gristle, let her look.* The Widow.

*Where's it gone,* said someone she didn't know well, a stoat-god from somewhere up the Sedge, and then forty people asked where it had gone at once, and the scroll behind her eyes went thick and fast as rain on a window.

Jess stood very still. Her hand went, as it always went, to the cord at her neck and the worn tin disc on it, Pennyfold tin, a hole punched through the middle and the face rubbed off by sixty years of thumbs. Twelve-Pence's penny. She didn't believe it was lucky. She held it anyway.

*She's doing the penny thing,* said Mothlight. *She's scared.*

"I'm not scared, I'm listening."

*Same thing in a bog,* said the Widow.

The mist moved. Not the way mist moves with the wind; the way a sheet moves when somebody under it sits up.

The bog-wight came out of the water about twelve feet in front of her and kept coming, longer than seemed fair. It was built out of the fen. That was the horror of wights, which the bounty notices never mentioned: they weren't monsters that lived in the peat, they were the peat, got up and walking, roots for sinews and black water running out of it in sheets, and in the middle of the face a hole where something had drowned once and was still trying to breathe. It smelled like a cellar that had been closed for a hundred years and opened all at once.

*OH,* said chat.

"There it is," said Jess, and her voice came out exactly as steady as she'd trained it to, because she'd been on stages since she was six and she knew that the audience can forgive anything but a cracked voice. "That's a wight. That's a big one. Gristle, you got your wish."

*BITE IT,* said Gristlemouth, with total sincerity, and with it came the heat, a tithe of his, spat across from wherever he was sitting in the Beyond: a bloom of red warmth under her ribs, an ache in her jaw as if her teeth wanted to be bigger. Gristlemouth tithed the way he talked, all at once and too much. *BITE IT IN THE FACE.*

"I am not biting it in the face, it's made of mud."

*Mud with a bit of pig in it,* said Mothlight. *Protein.*

The wight leaned. Peat-water poured off its arms. The hole in its face made a sound like a boot coming out of a bog, and it came for her.

She went left, which in that water meant she went nowhere quickly, and an arm the width of a barrel went past her shoulder and opened the fen where she'd been. Spray hit her in the face. It tasted of iron and old leaves. The scroll behind her eyes went to pure noise: names on names, *LEFT*, *MOVE*, *jess jess jess*, the Widow saying *steady, love, steady* under everything like a hand at her back, and a cold small steadiness did come with it, the Widow's own tithe, settling into her knees so they stopped shaking.

"Thank you," Jess said. "Thank you, that's lovely, all right." She got her feet under her. The wight was turning, slow as a barge. "Here's what we're going to do. We're going to ask the house."

*POLL,* said chat, delighted, because they always were.

"Two options. Do I punch it" — she raised her right fist; Gristlemouth's heat was still in it, her knuckles glowing faintly through the skin like coals through a grate — "or do I set it on fire? Punch, or fire. Punch or fire. Voting is open. You know the rules: you vote, you pay, and I do what you say."

The rules were not hers. She had learned them the way you learn the stairs in a dark house, by falling down them. Ask the house and the house answers, and every voice that answered put its own small portion of power behind its answer, and the answer that won came into her all at once. And if she then did the other thing, if the house said fire and she punched, all that power stayed in her with nowhere to go and turned round and went through her instead. She had done it once, in her first winter, out of pride. She had bled from the nose for a day and a half and been blind for one of them.

She had not done it since.

*punch!!* said Brindle, who always voted for whatever Jess had said first.

*PUNCH,* said Gristlemouth. *PUNCH IT AND THEN BITE IT.*

*Fire,* said the Widow firmly. *It's wet, dear. Your hand will go straight in.*

*Fire,* said Twelve-Pence. *A burned wight can be counted. A punched wight goes back in the bog and comes out Thursday.*

*Fire, obviously,* said Mothlight. *Punch is what she wants. Fire is what she needs. Character growth.*

The tally came up behind her eyes the way it always did, two columns of names racing each other, and the wight came up out of the water in front of her the way wights do, and for a second she watched both, and the fire column pulled away and kept pulling.

"Fire it is," she said. "The house has spoken."

It came in like weather. Every vote was a spark, and every spark was a different temperature: the stoat-god's quick and thin, Twelve-Pence's small and precise as a struck match, the Widow's slow and steady, a hundred others she couldn't have named, and Gristlemouth's, sulking, still voting punch, held back. They poured into her right hand together and her hand forgot it was a hand. Light came through the skin of her fingers and showed her own bones.

The wight reached for her.

Jess opened her hand in its face.

The fire went into the hole where something had drowned, and for one moment she saw, or thought she saw, the shape of what had drowned there, a small shape, a long time ago, and then the peat caught. Wights burned like turf fires: not fast, but all the way through. It went up in a column of white smoke and orange heart, roaring, and the roots that were its sinews cracked one after the other like knuckles, and it came down across the fen in a long collapse, hissing, and the water closed over most of it.

The scroll went mad. Names on names, a whole house on its feet.

*FIRE FIRE FIRE*

*good jess!! good!!*

*Fine,* said Gristlemouth. *That was fine. Fire's fine.*

*Seven out of ten,* said Mothlight. *I'm saving the three for the landing.*

"Thank you," said Jess, breathing hard, grinning, with smoke in her hair. "Thank you, you've been wonderful, I'm here all week —"

She stepped back, to bow, and the fen took her foot.

It was not dignified. She went over backwards with her arms out, and the peat-water closed over her face, cold and brown and tasting of dead leaves, and for a moment the scroll behind her eyes was the only light in the world. Two hundred and eighty-seven names, all laughing.

She came up spitting.

*AND THERE'S THE LANDING,* said Mothlight. *Ten. Ten out of ten. Perfect show.*

*Are you hurt? Are you hurt, love?*

*the wight is gone and jess is wet!! hello wet jess*

*Eight crowns and a florin,* said Twelve-Pence. *The boots are no longer a consideration.*

Jess lay back in the water, because there was no point getting up, and looked at the white lid of the mist and laughed until her ribs hurt. Then she found the penny on its cord and held it, and said, out loud, to the empty fen, in the voice she used for curtain calls, "Thank you. Honestly. Thank you, all of you. I couldn't have done it without you."

Which was true, and which she said every time, and which, from where the reeds stood thickest at the edge of the open water, looked like exactly what it was: a woman lying in a bog, thanking nobody.

The woman in the reeds had been watching for an hour. She wore Vigilant's grey under a waxed cloak, and a silver disc with a single open eye over her heart, and she had been tracking the warlock Jessamy Pell for three weeks across two counties. She had heard every word. She had seen the fire.

She waited until the warlock had stopped laughing. Then she wrote it down.
